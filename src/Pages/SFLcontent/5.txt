They ran until running stopped being a choice and became a rhythm.

Eva had expected to tire. She kept waiting for it, the way she had waited all of yesterday for her legs to fail her, and it did not come. The ground went by underneath in the dark, root and stone and leaf litter, and the body took it on its own. Beside her Zax ran without a sound. Behind them Anya's banked fire bobbed along like a ship's running light, and somewhere in the middle of it all Elara's small, fast footsteps kept time with nobody's.

Kenji flew ahead, low between the trunks, and came back every few minutes to report the same thing: still there. Still east. Closer.

After the first hour the forest began to change. The giant trees thinned and gave way to younger ones, and then to open ground, long grass silver under the moons. The land rose. Rock broke through the grass, then more rock, and then they were climbing a slope of loose stone that turned under their feet and went clattering away behind them into the dark.

Eva took the lead. Four legs, it turned out, were made for exactly this. She went up the scree in a series of short, sure bounds, and it was only at the top, with the wind suddenly in her face, that she realized she had not once looked down at her paws.

She crouched at the crest. One by one, the others came up beside her.

Nobody spoke. Below them, in the dark, was a town, and it was alive.

It lay in a shallow valley below the ridge, perhaps two kilometers off, along both banks of a slow river that caught the moonlight in long pale bands. Kenji's lines of light were there, just as he had said: lamps on posts along the paths and the riverbanks, amber points that looked from this distance like torches or oil lanterns. Only they did not flicker. Every one of them burned perfectly steady, like walkway lights on a starbase during the night shift.

And the buildings were wrong. Eva looked for a long time before she could say how.

There were no straight lines. There were trees, enormous ones, bigger even than the ones in the forest, and the houses were in them: windows set into the trunks, doors at the roots, rooms bulging out of the bark like burls on an old oak. Other houses stood on their own, low and rounded, their walls made of something woven, branches or vines grown tight together and roofed in living moss. Bridges crossed the river in three places. They were not built. They hung from tree to tree in long curves, and even from the ridge Eva could see leaves on them.

"No mortar," Anya said at last, very quietly. She was lying flat beside Eva with her chin on the rock. "No beams. No fasteners. They didn't put those up. They *grew* them." She was silent a moment. "The load paths are in the wood. They told the trees where to carry the weight." Another pause. "Captain, I want to see the drawings. I want to see whoever did the drawings."

"No roads," Kenji said. He had landed on Zax's shoulder and was leaning out as far as he could without falling off. "Paths. No roads, no vehicles, nothing with wheels. And there's no landing field. No pads, no lights on the rooftops. Nobody here flies anything." He considered. "Nobody here *needs* to fly anything."

"No wall," Zax said.

They looked at him.

"No wall. No ditch. No watchtower that I can see. The river is the only barrier, and it has three bridges across it." He was reading the edges of the town as if it were a sensor plot. "Either they have never been attacked, or they have another way of answering an attack that does not need one."

"Both possibilities are informative," T'Vella said.

"Only one of them is comforting."

Elara said nothing at all. She was looking at the windows, at the warm squares of light in the trunks of the trees. Eva did not need to ask what she was thinking. She was thinking it too. Someone was behind every one of those windows. Someone had lit that lamp, and was sitting in that room, and did not know that six strangers were lying on a ridge above them in the dark.

"We hold here," Eva said. "Nobody goes down until we have watched. I want to see who is awake at this hour and what they do. Zax, the far end. T'Vella, the river. Everyone else, eyes on the town. Quietly."

Zax let out a breath that might, in another face, have been a laugh. "My day on the ridge."

"A few minutes on the ridge. Don't get used to it."

Most of the town was asleep. The few who were still about all seemed to be going the same way: out of a lane of low, lamp-lit buildings near the middle bridge, where shutters were coming down one after another, and off toward the houses. A market street, closing for the night.

A shape crossed the nearest bridge with a basket on its back. It walked on two legs and was about Elara's size, and halfway across it stopped and leaned on the rail and looked at the water for a moment, as anyone might at the end of a long day. Behind it came a pair walking together, one large and four-legged, one small. The large one laughed at something. They could hear it from the ridge, faint and clear in the still air, and it was a laugh. There was no other word for it.

The small one glanced up at the ridge as it walked. Only a glance, the idle kind, the way anyone looks up at a hillside at night for no reason. Then it said something to its companion, and they went on over the bridge and out of sight.

Eva watched them go, and something in the back of her mind would not lie still.

They had spent two days in a forest full of animals. Squirrels and birds and things that watched from the trees. Four of them had tried to eat her that morning. And the shapes down there, the basket carrier and the pair on the bridge, were the same kind of shapes. The same sizes. The same build of ear and tail and limb. She could not have said where the line was between the squirrel that had run up the tree beside her and the figure leaning on the bridge rail to look at the river. There did not seem to be one.

And they were the same kind of shape too. The six of them. Lying on a ridge, looking down.

She did not say it. She saw, in the silence on either side of her, that she did not need to.

Beside her, Zax had not looked away from the town. But both his ears had turned, slowly, back toward the slope they had climbed.

"Captain," he said, very low. "Behind—"

"*Hold!*"

All six of them turned at once, and Eva felt her claws come out against the stone without being asked. Below them, at the top of the scree they had just climbed, three figures stood in the light of a small flame. They had come up the loose stone without a sound. They must have been following for a long time.

The one in front was small, smaller than Elara, a stocky, upright lizard with an orange hide and a cream-colored belly. Its tail curled up behind it, and on the tip of the tail a flame was burning, steady and bright, a little torch that lit its own face from below. Its eyes were fixed on Eva, and they were not afraid.

To its left stood something taller and sleeker. It was green, with a long, low head, and from each of its forearms grew a broad blade of a leaf, held out a little from the body as if ready to be used. Its eyes were yellow and narrow. It was looking at each of them in turn, and Eva had the uncomfortable sense that it was counting.

Behind the two of them, blotting out half the slope, was the third. It was enormous. It stood on two thick legs and its body was a heavy barrel of purple, and its head was mostly mouth, and around its head and shoulders were great round openings like the bells of horns, like speakers, like nothing Eva had a word for. It was not moving at all. Its head was tilted a little, like a dog's. It was listening.

Zax had moved without moving. His weight was on his back foot and one paw had drifted, slowly, toward the satchel on Anya's shoulder, toward the flap, toward the one thing in it that would answer this. Anya's fire had come back up by itself, a flare of orange along her neck that she pulled down again with a visible effort. Kenji had gone small and round on Zax's shoulder. Elara had both hands closed tight in front of her.

"Stand down," Eva said, without looking away. "Everyone. Stand down."

Zax's paw stopped. It did not come back. But it stopped.

The small one with the flame took a step up the slope toward them, and the light from its tail moved over them as it came: over Eva's horn, over Zax's mask, over the dull, scorched fabric of the satchel and the Starfleet buckle on its strap. Its gaze rested there a moment. Then it came back to Eva.

"We're from the Pathfinder Guild," it said. Its voice was clear and confident, and young. "We went out this morning to look at the silver streak that came down yesterday, just before sundown. We found it. A broken building made of metal, half-buried in a crater the size of a lake, with the glass still warm. It had all these… layers inside it. All smashed. And the air around it is wrong. It makes your fur stand up. We followed a trail away from it all day, and now we find the six of you, coming from the same direction, with the same wrong air on you." The flame on its tail gave a small, sharp flick. "So. Care to tell us who you are, and what you're doing sneaking up on our town in the middle of the night?"

For a moment nobody answered.

It was not the question. Eva had answers to questions like that ready; she had been working them out all day. It was something else, and it took her a full second to catch up with it, as it takes a second to notice that a sound has stopped.

She had understood him.

The sounds the little lizard had made were not words. She could hear that now, now that she was listening for it. They were chirps and growls, a crackle low in the throat, a rising note at the end of the question like a bird's call. Nothing about them was a language she had ever heard. And under them, at the same time, as clear as if he had spoken Standard on the bridge of the *Sagittarius*, was everything he had meant.

She had not translated anything. There had been nothing to translate. She had simply heard him, and known.

On Zax's shoulder, Kenji made a sound that was half a chirp and half a sob, and put a wing over his face. He had begun to shake. Zax, without looking, put a paw up and steadied him.

*They can talk,* Zax thought. Eva could see it on him as plainly as if he had said it aloud: the relief arriving, and the suspicion right behind it, hard on its heels. *They can talk. Which means they can lie.*

T'Vella had gone very still. *There was no lag,* she thought. *No processing. No sense of a second language being decoded. I did not understand him. I simply knew what he meant.*

Then a second thought arrived behind the first, and it was colder.

On the *Sagittarius* the universal translator had never been switched off. It ran in the ship's computer and in every combadge, all day, every day, and nobody gave it a thought. Zax swore at his console in Andorian. Anya talked to her warp core in Spanish. The captain, when she was tired, gave her orders in Russian, and T'Vella answered her in Vulcan, and every one of them heard all of it in their own language and never once noticed the seams.

The computer was a hole in the ground. Nobody had worn a combadge since the bridge.

And for two days, T'Vella had been speaking Vulcan.

She had not chosen to. She had simply spoken, the way she always had, and been answered. She looked along the line of them. At Anya, who had said *mi abuela* on the stream bank that morning, and been understood by everyone who heard it. At Kenji, whose despair over his own reflection had come to T'Vella as perfectly ordinary Vulcan, and had certainly not left his beak that way. Every word any of them had said since the crater had arrived in the language each of them was most at home in. No one had noticed, because nothing had changed.

Except that there was nothing left to do the translating.

She filed it, carefully, in the place she was keeping for the berries and the stream and the tree. *I do not know how,* she thought. *I would very much like to.*

Anya's mouth had dropped open a little. She was looking at Kenji, and Kenji, wing still half over his face, was looking back at her, and Eva could see the same arithmetic arriving on both of them at once.

"*Dios mío,*" Anya breathed, barely a sound. "I've been talking Spanish since the crater."

"Later," Eva murmured, without moving her eyes from the slope. "All of it. Later."

And Elara, small and silent at the back, had closed her eyes and put her fingertips to her temples. She was not listening to the words. Since the crash there had been a noise at the edge of her mind, like static on an open channel, and she had been trying all day not to notice it. She was noticing it now. Her brow creased. Then she opened her eyes and leaned a little toward Eva, and spoke so quietly that only Eva could hear.

"It's not clear," she whispered. "It's like listening through a bulkhead. I can't make anything out." She swallowed. "Except… I think they're scared, Captain. Like us."

Eva looked down the slope at the three of them. At the little flame on the lizard's tail, burning bright and steady in the dark, and at the green one's blade-arms held just a little out from its sides, and at the great, silent listener at the back. Three people sent out to find out what had fallen on their world and nearly burned it. Three people who had walked all day into air that made their fur stand up, and had found six strangers at the end of the trail.

Yes. She thought they probably were afraid.

"*Well?*"

The flame on the lizard's tail flared, sharp and white at the tip, and all six of them came back to the slope at once. "I asked you a question." It took another step up the scree. "You've been standing there whispering at each other for a full minute, like we can't see you do it. Who are you?"

There was so much she could tell them. That was the trouble. Everything was right there behind her teeth: the ship and the Federation, the warp core, the hundred and forty-one, the god in the sky. She could feel all of it wanting to come out at once, now that there was someone to tell.

She turned her head, just slightly, and met T'Vella's eyes.

T'Vella did not speak. She did not need to. She held Eva's gaze for one long moment, and then, so small that no one on the slope below could possibly have seen it, she shook her head. Once.

*I know,* Eva thought. *Nothing out of the satchel. Nothing they did not already have.*

She looked back down the slope.

"We are travelers," she said. Her voice came out level, and she was glad of it. "Our vessel crashed. That was our vessel, the thing you found. We are what is left of the people who were on it." She let that stand, because it was true and because she could not have kept the rest of it out of her voice if she had gone on. "We're survivors. We saw your lights, and we came looking for help."

The little lizard studied her. Its flame burned. Behind it, the big listener's head tilted the other way, as if it were hearing something in Eva's voice that the words had not said.

It was the green one that moved. It took one step forward, up onto the crest, and looked at them all again, one after another, slowly: Eva, Zax, the small bird on Zax's shoulder, Anya with her banked fire, T'Vella, Elara. Its narrow eyes came back to Eva and stayed there.

"Travelers," it said. "From where?" It shook its head. "No one's ever seen Pokémon like you before."

The word hung in the night air. Simple. Certain.

*Pokémon.*

None of them had ever heard it before. And yet, the moment it was spoken, it was not new. It sat in Eva's mind the way the name *Quick Attack* had sat there that morning, the way the knowledge that the blue berries were food had come to T'Vella the night before: something she had not been taught and somehow already had.

She looked at the six of them, crouched on the dark ridge. The Absol and the Lucario and the small grey bird, the great banked fire of the engineer, the tall green and white figure of her first officer, and Elara, smallest of all.

She looked down at the town, at the empty bridge, at the last lit doorways of the market street going dark one by one.

Then she looked back at the three on the slope.

It was a word for the creatures in the forest, and for the people in the town, and for the three standing below them in the dark. It was a word for all of them. It meant there was no line. She had been right about that, lying on the ridge.

And it was a word for the six of them now.

Eva Rostova had been a lot of things in her life. A cadet. A pilot. An officer, and then a captain, with a ship and a crew and a hundred and forty-one people who trusted her to bring them home. She had never been the thing someone else had a name for.

Beside her, Zax did not move. Kenji had stopped shaking. Anya's fire was very low. And T'Vella, who had a name for everything, said nothing at all.

They had come to this world as a crew. They had a name for that. They had been carrying it for two days like the satchel, like the dead emitter inside it, refusing to set it down.

The world, it seemed, had another name for them. And it had known that name all along.
