Relief went through the crew so strongly it was almost dizzying. They had a place. They had a purpose. For tonight, at least, they were safe.

"Excellent," said Guildmaster Ember-Smoke, and a slow smile spread across his old, rough face. "Every team needs a name. It is your banner, the call sign by which you will be known. Have you decided on one?"

"Team Sagittarius!" Kenji chirped, his loyalty unwavering.

"Absolutely not," Zax snarled, the name a fresh wound. "That name died with our crew. We will not dishonor them by carrying it as... this."

"But we should honor them," Anya countered, her voice thick with emotion. "We survived. We rose from the ashes. Team Phoenix?"

"A phoenix is reborn as it was," T'Vella stated, her logic a cold comfort. "We have not been reborn; we have been fundamentally altered. A name should reflect our current, illogical state."

Eva listened. The choice would come to rest with her; it always did. But this was not an order, and she was not going to give it like one. They were children of the stars, now fallen to a strange new earth.

"Starfall," she said. "We fell out of the sky, and we're still here." She looked around at them. "That's a suggestion, not a decision. Objections?"

Kenji's feathers drooped. "It's a little sad, Captain."

"It is," said Elara. She was quiet a moment. "But back home, when you saw a star fall, you made a wish on it." She looked at the others. "We're still here to make one."

Anya's fire came up a little along her neck. "A falling star still burns," she said slowly. "All the way down." She nodded. "I can live with that."

"It does not pretend nothing happened," said Zax. "And it does not wear their name." A short pause. "Agreed."

"It is accurate," said T'Vella, "and I find it is not illogical after all. I concur."

Kenji looked at them all, and at the floor, and back up. "Starfall," he tried. His chest feathers lifted a little. "Team Starfall. Okay. Yeah. That sounds like a team that's going somewhere."

Eva turned back to the Guildmaster. "Team Starfall," she said. "We are Team Starfall."

"Team Starfall," the Guildmaster repeated, nodding slowly. "A strong name. It speaks of a long journey and a new beginning." He gestured with his head towards a stack of fresh scrolls on a low table. "Then all that remains is the formality of registration."

Eva looked at the scroll. The script was row on row of small pictographs, a good many of them shaped like the prints of paws, claws and feet, and every one of them completely alien to her. She couldn't read a single character. However, the layout was universal. There were clear lines for names, titles, and a larger space at the bottom, presumably for a signature or mark. It was a form, and she knew how to fill out a form, even if she couldn't read the fine print.

"T'Vella," Eva said, her voice low. "You're on scribe duty."

T'Vella stepped forward, her Gardevoir form moving with a quiet grace. She took the pencil that lay beside the scrolls, a plain stick of wood with a blade-cut point like Leaf's, her slender fingers surprisingly adept. As Eva dictated the name, T'Vella carefully copied the sounds into the clean, angular letters of Federation Standard.

Ember-Smoke leaned forward, his old eyes studying the strange, efficient symbols. "A curious script," he commented mildly, "but I cannot read these markings. For the record, please state what you have written."

"Our apologies, Guildmaster," Eva said, gesturing to the scroll. "This is the script of our homeland. The first line is our team name: 'Team Starfall'."

"And the members?" he prompted.

Eva hesitated. The word ready on her tongue was *Captain*, and behind it the rest of the roster as it had stood on the *Sagittarius*: first officer, tactical, chief engineer, operations, helm. Every one of them belonged to a bridge. There was no bridge here, and no ship, and to an old Torkoal in a tree those words would mean nothing at best. At worst, they would raise questions she could not answer. She needed older words, plain ones, the kind an explorers' guild might have used long before anyone built a starship. Words for what each of them did.

She looked along the line of them, and found she already knew them.

"My name is Eva," she said. "Leader."

T'Vella's pencil scratched across the scroll.

"T'Vella, our Scholar." First officer and science officer both, and the one most likely to make sense of this world, if anyone could. T'Vella did not look up, but the pencil paused a moment before it moved.

"Zax, our Guardian." Under the phaser banks and the shield readouts, that was all a tactical officer had ever been: the one who stood between the crew and whatever came for it. Zax's ears went back, then slowly forward again, and he did not object.

"Anya, our Crafter." An engineer without an engine was still the one who made things work, out of whatever was to hand. Anya grinned at that.

"Elara, our Coordinator." She had kept the bridge talking to itself and to everyone else. She could keep the six of them together. Elara ducked her head, pleased.

"And Kenji, our Scout."

Kenji, who had flown a starship and now had only his own two wings, puffed up on Zax's shoulder until he was nearly round. "Scout," he whispered, as if it were a promotion.

T'Vella wrote the last line, and the scroll held six names in a script no one else on this world could read, each one spoken aloud for the Guild's record.

Ember-Smoke nodded slowly, absorbing the information. "I have never seen its like," he mused, looking at the finished document. "So precise. From a very distant land, indeed." If he was suspicious, he did not show it, and he did not press. Once the scroll was signed, he stamped it with a seal made from hardened sap, making it official. Team Starfall was born.

"You are dismissed," the Guildmaster said gently. "And unless I am very much mistaken, Cinder has not gone to bed either, and is waiting outside to show you to your quarters. Get some rest, Team Starfall. You have earned it."

The team gave a collective nod of gratitude and turned, opening the heavy wooden door. Cinder was waiting for them in the corridor, leaning against the curved wall of the great tree. He pushed himself upright as they emerged.

"He told me to go to bed," he said, before anyone could ask. "I will." He did not move. "So? What did he say?"

"We're a team!" Kenji burst out from Zax's shoulder. "A real one. Team Starfall!"

"Team Starfall," Cinder repeated. For a moment his eyes went somewhere else, and Eva had a fair idea where: the crater he had stood in that morning, and the metal in it. He did not say so. "Suits you," he said instead. Then it caught up with him. "Wait. He *registered* you? Tonight?"

"He did," said Eva.

"Just like that." Cinder looked from one of them to the next. "I thought he'd keep you talking till morning. Have Leaf write it all down twice. Make you wait a week, at least." His tail flame gave a flick. "What did you tell him?"

"What we could."

"Which is what?"

"Eva," said Zax quietly. Only her name, but she heard the rest of it.

"What was said in that room was said to the Guildmaster," said T'Vella. "It would be unwise to repeat it in a corridor."

Cinder's jaw set. "I tracked you all day," he said. "I walked you in past the front desk in the middle of the night. It's my team's name on that report. If you're trouble, I'd like to know what kind." He was not loud about it. He did not need to be.

Eva weighed it. He was not wrong. He had found them on a ridge in the dark and brought them in when he could just as easily have sent them on, and everything she had given him so far had been a lie, or near enough.

"We came a long way," she said. "We can't go back." She felt Zax's look on the side of her face, and let it stay there. "And there were more of us when we started."

That was all. Cinder heard where it stopped.

"...Oh," he said, a smaller sound than anything he had said yet.

Then he took a good look at them, properly this time, in the crystal light of the hallway. He saw the soot still clinging to Anya's fur, the way Kenji had sagged on Zax's shoulder now the excitement had run out of him, the deep, bone-weary exhaustion in all their eyes. He sighed. "The Guildmaster's given you one of the empty team rooms. It'll keep." He tipped his head down the corridor. "You all look like you're about to fall over. Food first. Come on."

Cinder went on ahead, his tail flame bobbing in front of them. Zax let the others pass and fell in at Eva's side, Kenji drooping on his shoulder.

"Nothing they did not already have," he said, low. Her own words, from the hillside.

"He stood in that crater this morning, Zax. He had most of it already." She kept her eyes on the light ahead. "All he has now is that we lost people."

Zax walked a few paces on that. "And when he asks for the rest?"

"Then we decide it together. Not in a corridor." She glanced up at him. "You said so. That's what I asked you for."

His ears eased, a little. It was not agreement, quite. But it would do, and they followed the light down.

The way down ended in the Guild's mess hall. At this hour it was nearly empty: a long, low room of tables and benches, lit by a few crystals turned low, with a hearth at the far end where a pot was being kept warm for the night shift. One table held a team just in from somewhere, three of them eating in tired silence. They looked up as Team Starfall came in, stared, and went back to their bowls. One lifted a paw to Cinder without looking up again.

The air was warm and thick with the rich scent of roasted chestnuts, the earthy aroma of a simmering mushroom stew, and the sharp, sweet tang of freshly pressed apples. Kenji's head came up off Zax's shoulder.

"Somebody actually *cooked* this," he breathed. "On a real fire."

Cinder gave him a look. "Where else would you cook it?"

"...Nowhere," said Kenji. "Good point." T'Vella's gaze rested on him for exactly one second.

Anya had already drifted toward the hearth. The pot hung from a hooked arm over a bed of coals, and the smoke went up into a hood of fired clay and away, somewhere up into the tree. "No fan, nothing," she murmured. "It's all in the shape of the flue." She sounded as if she would have liked to climb in and see how.

Team Starfall, a soot-stained island, took a table in the corner, too exhausted to care who was looking. Zax took the end of the bench that faced the door.

Cinder said a word to the cook, and the cook came out from behind the hearth to see who was eating at this hour. He was a Heatmor: red, long in the snout, and built like an old iron stove, with a vent at the tip of his tail that drew in air like a bellows whenever he breathed. A scorched cloth was tied around his middle.

"Braise," said Cinder. "They've been living on berries, by the look of them."

The Heatmor looked the six of them over once, without hurry, the way a smith looks over a bar of metal, and said nothing at all. Then bowls came: stew ladled thick from the pot, a heap of roasted chestnuts on a slab of slate, a jug of the pressed apple. After a moment's thought, a wide bowl of berries left over from the day, in colours none of them had ever seen.

"Leftovers," Cinder said, pushing the berries across. He named them as he went, a string of short, quick words that meant nothing to any of them, and not one of which stayed. Apples, at least, they knew.

Cinder sat down across from them, and his tail flame lit their corner better than the crystals did.

T'Vella regarded her bowl. "I would prefer to analyze this before I eat it."

The Heatmor had not gone back to his hearth. He stood at the end of the table, snout lowered toward T'Vella's bowl, and waited.

"The tricorder can't spare it," Anya murmured.

"I am aware." T'Vella looked at the stew a moment longer, as though it might volunteer its composition. Then, under the Heatmor's steady regard, she ate.

The food was a revelation. For the first time since the crash, they tasted something other than blue berries and fear. Kenji had his whole face in his bowl.

Braise watched it reach each of their faces in turn, and whatever he saw there seemed to satisfy him. He took a handful of raw chestnuts from a pocket in his cloth, spilled them onto the slate, bent his snout over them, and breathed out. A thin tongue of flame ran over the shells, steady and exact as a cutting torch. They split with small, sharp cracks, and the smell of them filled the corner.

Anya stopped chewing. She had stood beside good welders all her working life, and she watched the flame the way she had watched them: for the control in it more than the fire. "How hot do you run that?"

The flame went out. "Hot enough," said Braise.

Anya was still looking at the slate. Every shell had split clean along its seam, and not one of them was scorched. Her paw went up, without her quite meaning it to, to the flames along her own neck: the fire she had carried since the crater, that flared when she was angry and sank when she grieved and had only ever answered to her moods.

"Can..." Her voice came out smaller than she meant it to. "Can I do that?"

Braise looked at her then, properly: at the fire on her neck, and at her face.

"Not like that," he said. He pushed the slate toward her. "Not yet."

Then he went back to his hearth, and Anya sat a while with a chestnut in her paw, not eating it, watching him go.

Elara kept glancing at the team across the room. One of them had a wing bound up in a strip of cloth; another had fallen asleep sitting up, chin on the table. Whatever their day had been, it had been long. She wanted to go over and ask if they were all right. She did not. They looked as if they only wanted to be left to eat, and she knew the feeling.

Then the berries. Elara tried a soft pink one, went very still, and reached for another before she had finished the first.

Zax took a small red one, bit down, and stopped chewing.

Anya watched him. "You okay there?"

"Adequate," said Zax. He finished it. Then he took another.

"Your eyes are watering."

"They are not."

Behind the hearth, the Heatmor had seen. A little later a small dish arrived at Zax's elbow with nothing in it but red ones. By the time Zax looked up, Braise was already back at his pot.

Zax regarded the dish for a long moment, the way he regarded unattended doors. Then he moved it a little closer, and took one.

Anya's chestnut stopped halfway to her mouth. "No way..."

Kenji pulled his face out of his bowl and leaned round to look. "Is that... Zax, is your tail *wagging*?"

It was. The tail Zax had given no thought to since the crater was sweeping back and forth across the bench behind him in short, happy strokes.

"It is not," said Zax.

He reached back and clamped it flat against the bench with one paw. It went on twitching under his paw, which was somehow worse.

"Your eyes are watering *and* your tail's wagging," said Anya. "Over a berry."

"It is a reflex. To the heat."

"Mm-hm."

"Fascinating," said T'Vella, without looking up. Eva looked very hard at her stew.

Zax, with great dignity, ate another, and kept his paw where it was.

"So how big is the Guild?" Kenji asked, loudly enough that every head at the table turned to Cinder instead. "How many teams are there? Where do they all go?"

Under cover of the question, Zax's paw came off his tail. He did not look at Kenji. Kenji, very carefully, did not look at him either.

If Cinder had seen any of it, he let it go. "More teams than rooms, some seasons," he said through a yawn. "Never all home at once. Some go out for a day, some for a month. Escorts, deliveries, finding folk who went into a dungeon and didn't come out." He shrugged. "Wherever we're asked."

"And them?" Elara asked softly, with a glance at the table across the room.

Cinder looked. "Escort job over the hills. Three days out, and it shows." He turned back. "They'll be fine. They'll sleep till noon."

Elara let out a breath, and went back to her bowl.

"And at night?" said Zax. "Who stands watch?"

"Over the Guild?" Cinder's tail flame gave an amused flicker. "Nobody's been fool enough to try anything in here in my lifetime. And Rumble sleeps light. He can hear a door open three halls away."

Zax considered that, and seemed to find it acceptable.

Eva listened the way she had listened to a thousand briefings, filing each answer away. It was not much. But it was a structure, and she knew what to do with one of those.

Now and then Cinder looked at the six of them over the table, the way he had looked back from the Guildmaster's door, and said nothing.

The talk ran down somewhere in the second bowl. Kenji's questions came slower, then stopped. They finished in a deep, easy quiet, the simple act of a hot, nourishing meal a more potent comfort than any words could offer.

After their meal, their bellies full and the worst of their fatigue abated, Cinder led them back out into the atrium. The boards stood dark in the middle of the floor, the great map a shadow overhead, and at the desk by the door the bird was asleep again with its head under its wing. Cinder went past it on his toes.

He took them into the hall on the far side, opposite the one that led to the mess hall. It curved as it went, following the round of the trunk, and sloped down so gently that Eva only noticed it by the air: cooler with every turn, and smelling more and more of damp earth and moss. The carved walls gave way to rougher wood, and then to wood that was no longer trunk at all but root, great twisted cables of it running overhead and down into the floor. They were below ground now, somewhere beneath the tree. Doors were set into the roots on either side, and over most of them a name had been carved.

Cinder stopped before a heavy wooden door set into the side of a thick, living root as wide as a shuttlecraft. Nothing had been carved over this one yet. "This will be your team base for now," he said, his voice echoing slightly in the quiet hall. "The rooms down here are for the newer teams. They're not much, but they're dry, safe, and far enough away from the main hall that you won't be woken up by the night-shift teams coming and going."

He opened the door to reveal a single, large, circular chamber. The floor was covered in soft, sweet-smelling moss. Arranged around the edge were six round, woven-straw beds. In one corner, a sturdy wooden desk had been carved right out of the wall, and a small, curtained-off alcove revealed a few empty wooden chests for storage. A single, glowing crystal in the ceiling provided a soft, warm light. It was simple, rustic, and the most beautiful sight they had ever seen.

"Thank you, Cinder," Eva said, her voice thick with genuine gratitude.

The Charmander just nodded. "Get some rest, Team Starfall. Tomorrow, your new life begins."

He left, closing the door behind him, and the room went quiet. For a long moment, the six of them just stood there, taking it all in. Zax walked to the door, testing its weight and the simple wooden lock, and grunted, satisfied. Anya, meanwhile, set their single survival satchel on the floor. With a quiet sigh, she reached inside and pulled out the dead shelter emitter, its casing cool and familiar. It had not worked since the morning before, but it was still a solid piece of Starfleet engineering, a ghost of their old life. She placed it carefully under the desk, out of the way, before running a claw over the smooth surface of the desk itself, an engineer appreciating solid craftsmanship. Kenji fluttered down and immediately began fluffing one of the straw beds, chirping contentedly.

"We're safe," Eva said softly, and for the first time since the crash it sounded true.

"Our probability of long-term survival has increased by approximately 87.4 percent," T'Vella added, a hint of what might have been relief in her melodic voice.

Anya approached one of the beds. At first glance, it looked like a giant, oversized bird's nest, woven from thick, fragrant straw and reeds. "Primitive, but functional," she muttered to herself. It seemed crude and uncomfortable, but as she curled up in it, the woven material gave way with a soft rustle. The straw was filled with something soft and yielding—perhaps dried moss or cattail fluff—that shifted and molded to her hulking Typhlosion form like a high-tech memory foam mattress or a bean bag chair. After a few wiggles to get settled, she found it was surprisingly, deeply comfortable. "Remarkable," she murmured, impressed by the simple, effective engineering. Her flames dimmed to a gentle, hearth-like glow that filled the space with warmth.

Zax, ever the tactical officer, chose a bed near the door. He tested it warily, but found the same unexpected comfort. He settled in a low crouch, a position he could spring from in an instant, but the softness of the bed was already lulling his tense muscles into a state of relaxation he hadn't felt in days. Elara practically melted into her bed with a soft sigh. For Kenji, the bed was perfect. "It's a nest!" he chirped happily, burrowing into the straw and fluff until only the top of his head was visible. T'Vella analyzed her own bed with a scientific curiosity. "A single, simple design that can ergonomically accommodate a wide variety of drastically different physiologies. The engineering is illogical, yet undeniably effective." One by one, the others discovered the same strange comfort in their own nest-like beds, the weariness of days finally claiming them as they found their own perfect sleeping positions.

Eva watched them, her family, safe and sheltered at last. They were no longer on the bridge of a starship, but in a room inside a tree. They were no longer Starfleet officers, but members of Team Starfall. It was a world away from everything they had ever known, but for the first time since the light had torn them from their reality, it felt like home. She lay down at last, and sleep took her before she could think to resist it.

For a while there was only darkness, and the straw, and the slow give of her own body. Muscles she had held tight for days let go one at a time: her shoulders, then the long line of her back, then her paws. Somewhere above her the great tree creaked in the wind, the way it had in the atrium. Like a ship, she had thought then.

It went on creaking. Under it, so low she felt it more than heard it, something began to hum.

She knew that hum. She tried to lift her head to place it, and found she could not. Nothing answered her: not her legs, not her tail, not her eyes, which would not open. Far away a tone sounded, two notes, rising, and stopped, and sounded again, closer. Red light came and went on the inside of her eyelids.

Then the creak was a hull under strain, and the hum was a warp core, and she was in her chair on the bridge, and she could move.

"Zax, target that creature's energy source! Full phaser spread!" she commanded, her voice raw. Her human crew—her real crew—were at their stations, their faces illuminated by the frantic, pulsing red of the alert lights.

"Trying, Captain!" the Andorian tactical officer yelled back, his antennae twitching wildly as he fought a dying console. "The power conduits are unstable! I can't get a clean lock!"

"Anya, I need more power to the shields!"

"They're gone, Captain! The emitters are slag!" the engineer's voice screamed over the comm, then dissolved into static.

The ship lurched, a sickening, violent shudder that wasn't from an external impact. The inertial dampeners had failed. Eva felt a phantom sensation of weightlessness, then crushing gravity as her own human form was thrown against her restraints. Before she could react, the serpentine beast on the screen unleashed its attack. A beam of pure, golden energy slammed into the saucer section. The ship screamed, and consoles exploded. She watched in horror as the helm station detonated, the acrid smell of ozone filling her nostrils as her brilliant helmsman, Lieutenant Tanaka, was thrown from his seat like a rag doll.

"All hands, abandon ship! I repeat, abandon—" Eva's command was cut short as Zax finally got a lock.

"Firing phasers!" he yelled. Two beams of orange light lanced out, followed by the thump of a torpedo leaving the tube. The attacks struck the creature, scorching its scales and staggering it for a moment, but it was like throwing stones at a mountain. The beast roared, its anger now focused, absolute. It gathered another, larger sphere of energy.

"It's targeting engineering," Anya's voice crackled, filled with dread. "The warp core..."

The final beam struck. The bridge lights died, plunging them into a terrifying darkness lit only by the red alert strobes and the brilliant, blue-white glow emanating from the viewscreen—the light of their own annihilation. A piercing, world-ending sound screamed through the bridge—

Eva's eyes snapped open, her heart hammering against her ribs. She was ready to scream an order, to brace for impact, but the sound wasn't a klaxon. It was the gentle, melodic morning bell of the Guild. She was in her straw bed, safe. The rest of her crew, her new crew, were beginning to stir. It had not even happened like that. By the time the serpent came, the bridge had been empty, and she had been standing in a forest, watching. She quickly turned, burying her face in the soft, yielding straw, forcing her breathing to even out while the cold shame of it settled in her chest.

"Morning muster?" Kenji chirped, shaking the straw from his feathers.

As Eva pushed herself up, she caught Zax's gaze from across the room—a brief, questioning look. Zax missed very little; she knew he had sensed something. The feeling of being seen, of her mask slipping for even a second, was all the motivation she needed to lock it firmly back into place.

"Unknown," she stated, her voice remarkably steady. She nodded towards the door, where T'Vella was already observing the hallway. "T'Vella, status?"

"There is a significant increase in foot traffic, Captain," the Gardevoir replied. "All personnel appear to be moving in a coordinated direction."

"When in doubt, follow the crowd," Eva decided. "Let's not be late for whatever this is."

They emerged from their quarters to find the halls of the great tree buzzing with energy. Dozens of exploration teams were streaming past, some chatting excitedly, others still shaking off sleep, but all heading the same way: up toward the atrium. Team Starfall fell into the living current, their presence still drawing curious glances, but no one stopped them. They were just another team heading to the morning briefing.

As they entered the main chamber of the Guild, they saw Cinder leaning against a support pillar, polishing the winged badge on his strap. He spotted them and gave a nod of approval.

"Figured it out on your own, huh?" he said with a smirk. "Good instincts. Some new teams spend their first week sleeping through the bell."

"Standard operating procedure," Zax grunted, though he felt a small measure of pride. "Observe and adapt."

Before they could continue, Anya, who had been carrying the tricorder, stopped dead. "Captain," she said, her voice filled with a quiet excitement. She held up the device. It had run almost flat the day before, spent on berries and scorch marks and one timed minute of Zax's light, and she had kept it switched off since to save what was left. Now the cracked screen showed a faint, flickering power bar with one more segment lit than last night, glowing a hopeful green.

"Something charged it," she said. "Overnight."

T'Vella leaned in, her red eyes on the screen. "From what source?"

"That's what I want to know." Anya tapped her way into the power logs with the tip of a claw. Even switched off, the cell kept a record of everything that went into it, and there it was: a thin, steady inductive input, starting not long after they had gone to bed and running all night. Then a few short blips, and then nothing. She checked the time on the last one. "It stopped when we walked out of that room. The blips are the hall on the way up."

"Then the source was in the room," said T'Vella. "And along the hall. But not here."

Anya turned slowly on the spot with the tricorder held out in front of her, and stopped facing the nearest wall, where one of the light crystals sat set into the wood. She took a step toward it. Then another. At arm's length from the stone, a new line opened in the log: the same input, on the same frequency, thin but steady.

She stepped back. It stopped. She stepped in. It came back.

"The crystals," she breathed. "Down in that room we were close enough. Out here, we aren't."

"Fascinating," the Vulcan observed. "Then they are not only producing light. They are emitting a low-level, resonant field on a frequency the tricorder's induction charging can accept, and it falls off sharply with distance." She straightened. "It is inefficient. But it is enough."

It was a lifeline: a link to their science and their methods, and a foothold.

"Anya, T'Vella, that's brilliant," Eva said, her mind already calculating the possibilities. "Keep it exposed to the crystals whenever possible."

Their conversation was cut short as Guildmaster Ember-Smoke made his way to a raised platform at the front of the hall. A respectful silence fell over the assembled teams.

"Good morning, Pathfinders!" the old Torkoal's voice carried through the vast space. "Another beautiful day in Havenwood! The Kecleon market has a fresh stock of Reviver Seeds, so stock up before your journeys!"

He continued, outlining minor news and events before turning to the large mission board behind him. "Now, for today's tasks!" As he spoke, old professional habits took over.

"We have reports of an unusually aggressive swarm of Beedrill in the Silent Woods..."

Zax's ears went forward at the word *swarm*. For a moment he was back under a sky gone dark with Hur'q, a phaser rifle hot in his hands. They had come like weather, and every one that dropped had three more behind it. He still heard their wings, some nights.

He had no rifle now. His mind went to work anyway, the way it always did: choke points, a fighting withdrawal, swarm dynamics. He found he did not want the job.

"...a request for escort services for a merchant heading to the coast..." Elara considered the logistics, the variables of protecting a non-combatant civilian in potentially hostile territory.

"...and a fascinating geological survey mission in the Crystal Caves." T'Vella's head came up, the mention of unique geological phenomena immediately capturing her scientific curiosity.

Eva watched it all, her mind sharp and analytical. She saw the flow of information, the allocation of resources, the clear chain of command. This was a system she understood. It was, in its own way, very much like Starfleet.

"And finally," the Guildmaster said, his old eyes seeming to find theirs in the crowd. "A task for our newest recruits. Team Starfall, please see me after the briefing."

A murmur went through the crowd. All eyes turned to them.

As Guildmaster Ember-Smoke finished his main announcements and retreated from the platform, the Chatot from the reception desk fluttered up, a comically large stack of scrolls in his claws. "Alright, settle down, settle down!" he squawked, his voice sharp and piercing. He was Chirp, the Guild's long-suffering administrator, and this was his domain. His eye found Team Starfall in the crowd and narrowed: the six who had walked straight past his desk in the middle of the night. "But before we get to the daily postings, let's give a warm Havenwood welcome to our newest recruits, Team Starfall!"

A smattering of applause and a few cheerful "Hoorahs!" echoed through the hall, and one "*HOORAH!*" so loud it rattled the papers on the boards: Rumble, who by daylight saw no reason to hold back. Cinder's team gave them a nod of professional acknowledgement.

"Alright, that's enough of that!" Chirp continued, his voice shifting into a rapid-fire, practiced cadence as he unfurled his list. "Time for the daily tasks! Let's see... a request from Spinda's Café for six Pecha berries, one lost pearl retrieval for a very distraught Shellder, a request to polish the town's light crystals..."

He continued on, his recitation of the minor errands and low-risk tasks a familiar, weary routine. Boredom settled over the main hall. The initial energy of the morning briefing dissipated, replaced by restless shuffling. Zax noticed a large Snorlax from another team, who had been leaning against a wall, was now sound asleep, a small bubble inflating and deflating from its nose with each thunderous snore.

"This part of the briefing appears to be... less than critical," T'Vella observed quietly, her logical mind struggling to find the efficiency in the process.

"It's for the rookies," Cinder whispered to them, having sidled over. "Gives the new teams a way to earn some coin and build a reputation without getting themselves flattened in a Mystery Dungeon. We all started with berry-picking."

*Mystery Dungeon.* Eva had heard the words once before, last night, from the Guildmaster: the place lost wanderers came out of with their memories gone. He had said it as though it explained itself.

"Define 'Mystery Dungeon,'" T'Vella murmured.

Cinder looked at her. Then something in his face settled, as if a piece had dropped into place. "Right," he said. "Your memories." He scratched the back of his head with a claw. "It's... not a thing you explain, really. You'll see one soon enough."

"How soon?" Kenji whispered, a little too eagerly.

"Soon enough," said Cinder.

Finally, after what felt like an eternity, the Chatot chirped, "And that's the lot! Dismissed!"

The hall erupted into motion as teams dispersed, some heading for the exit, others crowding the mission board to claim the more exciting jobs. The only one who didn't move was the Snorlax, who continued his nap, oblivious. His teammates, a pair of much smaller Mankey, sighed in unison and began trying to shove their colossal partner awake.

"Team Starfall," Cinder said, gesturing with his head. "The Guildmaster is waiting."

They navigated the bustling room and approached the platform where Ember-Smoke was conversing quietly with his Chatot assistant. The old Torkoal turned his slow, steady gaze upon them as they arrived.

"Ah, Team Starfall," he said. "I trust your first night in Havenwood was satisfactory?"

"It was, Guildmaster. We are grateful for your hospitality," Eva replied respectfully.

"Good. Hospitality is easy. Trust is earned," the Guildmaster stated, his tone shifting from pleasantry to business. "Every new team starts with a simple task. It allows us to gauge your abilities, your teamwork, and your character. It is a test."

He gestured with his head towards the exit. "Just east of Havenwood is a small forest known as the Murky Wood. Recently, the wild Pokémon there have become unusually timid and frightened. This fear... it is new. It began only a few days ago. They are hiding, and food is growing scarce for them. This is causing an imbalance."

A first official mission. Eva knew the feeling. The Guildmaster's assignment was logical, a test not of brute force, but of observation and discipline. She met his gaze, her expression becoming a neutral mask of professionalism. Her team was an unknown quantity, and this was his way of getting a baseline reading on their competence.

He continued, his voice steady. "Your mission is simple. Go to the Murky Wood. Discover the source of the Pokémon's fear. Do not engage it directly. Your task is reconnaissance only. Observe, analyze, and report back to me. Cinder's team has already cleared a path to the entrance. You will not face any significant threats on the way."

Observation, data collection, and non-interference unless absolutely necessary: a classic Starfleet mission profile.

"We understand, Guildmaster," Eva confirmed. "Reconnaissance only. We will not let you down."

"See that you don't," Ember-Smoke said. "Now go. Your first assignment awaits."

With a shared look of grim determination, Team Starfall turned. As they walked back through the now-bustling atrium, daylight falling softly from the skylights high in the trunk, they truly took in their surroundings for the first time. The map they had glimpsed in the dark was spread across the whole curve of the ceiling, charting vast coastlines and towering mountain ranges, and smaller ones were etched into the walls between the halls. Anya, the engineer, found her eyes drawn to a complex cutaway diagram of what looked like a cave system, but a frustrated growl escaped her as she realized the detailed technical notes beside it were meaningless symbols.

"Captain," T'Vella stated, her voice a low, analytical hum. "An observation. It was not only last night's form. Our auditory processing has adapted to the local language, but it appears our optical character recognition has not. We are, for all intents and purposes, illiterate."

The rest of the team paused, looking at the beautiful, incomprehensible script labeling the maps around them. The names of forests, caves, and mountain passes—all of it was alien.

"We can't read a signpost," Zax grunted, the tactical disadvantage of the situation hitting him with full force. "We're operating blind."

They stepped out of the Guildhall into the bright sun, carrying their first mission and the daunting fact of their own ignorance. Their new life had truly begun.
