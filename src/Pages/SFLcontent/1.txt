*Captain's Log, Stardate 88412.6. The U.S.S. Sagittarius is en route to the Althos system, our final staging point before we begin our primary mission: a two-year deep-space survey of the uncharted Althos Expanse. We sail on special assignment from Fleet Admiral Veris, with a personal note that read, in full, "Take the Sagittarius. She'll surprise you." She is an odd vessel for the job, and I would not trade her. The Springfield-class spaceframe is the better part of fifty years old and out of date on paper, held together by a frankly ridiculous refit, every piece of it the Admiral's choosing, that three different yards have told me should not work in one hull. It does. Chief Sharma spent her first week aboard looking for the rest of her engine room. Most of main engineering is a bank of isomagnetic plasma distribution manifolds, each one there to push more power into the phasers than this hull was ever rated for. Tactical carries universal modules lifted from a dozen other classes, and nearly all of them feed the phasers too. The broadside combat module along our flanks was built for a dreadnought. Turn her side to an enemy and the hull behind the shields hardens; give the order and both flanks open at once, ten volleys in five seconds at up to five ships a side, every hit knocking their impulse engines offline. It answers to the bridge alone. I have yet to need it, and I am told that is the point. Even the torpedoes are agony phaser torpedoes, drawing on the same power as the arrays. Almost everything aboard feeds the phasers. Lieutenant Zax walked the weapons deck once, said nothing for a full minute, and has been in an excellent mood ever since. Science was not forgotten: the mission pod between the nacelles carries a sensor suite built to find the weak point in an enemy's shields at half a light-year, with enough exotic particle generation to make Commander T'Vella raise an eyebrow. A system designed to pick a warship apart, it turns out, is just as good at picking apart a nebula. Should the Expanse prove unfriendly, this small ship hits like something three times her size. Morale is high, and the crew is eager to put our names on the star charts. There is a quiet satisfaction in these long, silent stretches between the stars. They remind us what Starfleet is for: not just to go, but to see. Rostova out.*

The hum of the U.S.S. Sagittarius's warp drive was a constant, comforting symphony to its crew: the reliable thrum of a Springfield-class light explorer, three hundred and twenty-five meters of old spaceframe. Newer, larger vessels held the front lines now. The Sagittarius did what her class had always done best: she went to the edge of the map and looked over it. On the bridge, Captain Eva Rostova sat in her command chair, her eyes on the swirling tunnel of warp space on the main viewscreen.

"Status report," she said, her voice cutting cleanly through the low thrum of the ship.

"All systems nominal, Captain," responded First Officer T'Vella from her right. The Vulcan's face was a mask of serene logic, her pointed ears twitching slightly, the only sign of her constant, vigilant analysis of the ship's data streams. "We are maintaining Warp 7. ETA to the Althos system is 48 hours."

At the helm, Lieutenant Kenji Tanaka grinned. With the ship holding steady at warp there was little for a pilot to do but watch, and he had been tabbing idly through the performance screens: field symmetry, nacelle temperatures, the long flat line of the power curve. "Smooth sailing, Captain. The Sagittarius is flying like a dream." He was young, eager, and one of the best pilots in Starfleet. Something every other pilot in Starfleet has said about themselves too.

"That's because I've personally calibrated every plasma injector myself, Kenji," a cheerful voice with a warm Spanish lilt crackled over the comm. "Treat her well, or you'll have me to answer to."

Kenji grinned. "Wouldn't dream of it, Chief Sharma. She's in good hands."

"Let's hope it stays that way," muttered Tactical Officer Zax from his station at the rear of the bridge. His antennae twitched with restless energy. "I've got a bad feeling about this sector. Too quiet."

"The probability of an external threat manifesting without any prior energy readings is 0.0013 percent, Lieutenant," T'Vella stated, her voice a placid sea of logic. "Your feelings are statistically insignificant."

"Tell that to the Gorn warship that decloaked on top of the *Lexington*," Zax shot back. "I'm sure their statistical analysis was flawless right up until the torpedoes hit."

"An Andorian's intuition is a valuable asset, Commander," Operations Officer Elara Vance added softly from her station, not looking up from her console. "It's a form of subconscious pattern recognition. The silence itself is a pattern."

T'Vella's ear twitched, a silent concession to Elara's point.

"Alright, you two," Captain Rostova started, a hint of amusement in her voice. "Let's not start a philosophical debate on the merits of logic versus intuition. Zax, quantify 'too quiet' for me. Elara, anything on long-range sensors?"

Zax's antennae twitched again. "No active scans, no ship signatures, not even a stray asteroid. It's like this entire sector was wiped clean. It's unnatural."

Elara shook her head, her brow furrowed in concentration. "He's right, Captain. The background subspace chatter is unusually low. It's like listening to static in a soundproofed room. It's there, but it's... muted."

It started with a single tone: a thin, rising whine from the long-range sensor station, one subsystem raising its hand. Navigation answered it a beat later. Then they all came.

The structural integrity board came in on top of them in rapid triplets. The master caution at the helm lit amber, then red, and set up a two-note bray that did not stop. Ops threw up an energy surge across every band at once and added a warble of its own; the sensor board answered with a stuttering pulse; over the comm, faint and frantic, came engineering's alarm. Each layered over the last until the bridge rang with a chord of overlapping tones no one could pick apart. Red alert came on by itself. The lights dropped to crimson and began to pulse.

And on the viewscreen, dead ahead, the blue-white tunnel of warp space had a flaw in it: a single point of gold, very small, shimmering, and growing.

"What was that!?" Rostova demanded, gripping the arms of her chair as the deck began to shudder.

"I- I don't know!" Tanaka yelled, fighting the helm, trying to drop out of warp, change heading, anything. Every screen said the ship was responding, impulse spooling back to half, and still they kept going. "The ship is not responding! Unable to drop from warp!"

The viewscreen distorted violently. The familiar blue-white tunnel of warp space was shifting in color, that point of gold enveloping the entire screen in no time, those golden swirls shifting further into a chaotic spiral of impossible colors. A blinding, pure white light erupted from the center of the screen, engulfing the bridge. It wasn't an explosion; it was an arrival. An energy wave, unlike anything Starfleet had ever recorded, washed over the ship. It wasn't destructive, but it was overwhelming, a psychic and physical shockwave that bypassed the shields as if they weren't there.

The bridge became a cacophony of dying systems. Annunciator panels flashed from red to amber to black as entire sub-systems failed in unison. "I've lost helm control!" Kenji shouted, his hands flying over a console whose readings made no sense: velocity pinned at warp 9.5, angular velocity swinging left and right at once, and no amount of pulling the throttles back helped.

"Navigational shielding is fluctuating! I can't compensate!" Zax frantically tried to get a lock on the source of the energy wave, but his tactical display was a meaningless waterfall of sensor data of all types, the vibrations being sent through the vessel not helping his ability to focus.

"I have no readings, Captain! It's... it's everything!" T'Vella's fingers flew across her science station, attempting to analyze the phenomenon. "The wave is exhibiting properties of both psionic and temporal energy, yet it conforms to neither!" she stated, her voice strained for the first time as her console’s readings were no better than Zax’s.

Elara cried out, her hands flying to her temples as the psychic component of the wave washed over her, a deafening roar of a billion alien thoughts and noises overwhelmed her. One by one, the crew succumbed in similar fashions. Kenji slumped forward first, his fight with the dead helm lost. Zax fell back in his chair, his antennae drooping. Elara collapsed silently. T'Vella's disciplined mind held out a moment longer before she too went limp, her fingers frozen over her console.

Captain Rostova held on. She did not know how; she only knew that the chair was hers and she was still in it, gripping its arms while the bridge roared around her and the edges of her vision went to grey. On the viewscreen, the comm window flickered open from engineering, and there was Anya Sharma, her eyes wide with terror, clutching her head.

Then the golden chaos tore away, all at once, like a curtain, and the viewscreen cleared.

There were no stars. There was a world. Green and brown and swirled with white, it filled the screen edge to edge, and it was close, far too close, its curve already flattening into a horizon as the Sagittarius fell out of warp straight at it. Somewhere in the dying bridge a new alarm began, the one she had only ever heard in simulations. *Proximity alert.*

*Too close,* she thought, with perfect clarity, and her hand went to the override panel on the armrest, the captain's console that could take the helm from here. The screen lit under her palm. It made no sense. The controls she had used a thousand times were in the wrong places, or the right places and she could not read them, the symbols sliding away from her eyes faster than she could follow. Her hand hovered over it, shaking, trying to find the one command that mattered. Her eyes blurred, and her world, too, went black.

…

Consciousness returned slowly, like swimming up from a deep, dark ocean. Eva Rostova’s first sensation was of soft grass beneath her. Her last memory was of the bridge, the alarms, the gold light. As her senses came online, she became aware of a gentle warmth on her skin and a faint, golden luminescence that was slowly fading from the air around her. In the distance, she could hear a faint, rhythmic wail—the red alert siren of the Sagittarius, muffled by distance and the dense foliage.

*A transporter, then,* she thought, her mind grasping for a logical explanation. *Someone must have initiated an emergency site-to-site transport. Evacuated the bridge crew first. Standard protocol in a catastrophic failure.* The fading golden light felt like any transporter beam letting go: a faint tingle along her skin, the last of the shimmer thinning to nothing. She had felt it a thousand times. She pushed herself up, her limbs feeling strangely heavy and uncoordinated. She looked down at her hands—or where her hands should have been. In their place were two powerful, feline paws, covered in sleek, white fur. A jolt of pure, unadulterated shock shot through her. She tried to cry out, but the sound that escaped her throat was a guttural, feline yowl. Her eyes, now possessing the sharp clarity of a predator, darted around. She was in a forest, sunlight dappling through a thick canopy of oversized leaves. She was an Absol.

A groan nearby drew her attention. T'Vella was stirring, the last vestiges of the same golden light fading from around her new, graceful Gardevoir form. The Vulcan's logical mind, for the first time in her life, was utterly overwhelmed by the sheer impossibility of it all. Scattered around her, each in their own fading nimbus of golden light, were the others.

"Captain...?" T'Vella's voice was a soft, melodic chime, a stark contrast to her usual measured tones. "Emergency transport... but the bio-transformation is... illogical." She’d respond, looking over her new form with great curiosity.

Before Eva could respond, a frantic chirping sound erupted from a nearby patch of light. Kenji Tanaka, the ace pilot, was now a small, feathery Starly, hopping in panicked circles, flapping his new wings uselessly.

Zax, the ever-vigilant tactical officer, was perhaps the most fittingly transformed. He was a Lucario, his Andorian antennae replaced by four long, black appendages on the back of his head. He stood on two legs, his new form shaking as he finally looked at his arms, then himself in general. “Wh- What is this…?”

Elara Vance, the operations officer, had become a Kirlia, her small, humanoid form trembling as she took in the impossible scene, eyes wide as she looked over herself, her new size certainly being the most concerning change to her.

And from another fading glow, a low growl rumbled. Anya Sharma, the brilliant engineer, was now a hulking, bipedal Typhlosion, a collar of fire flickering nervously around the back of her neck.

As they slowly, shakily gathered, the distant wail of the sirens was joined by a low, persistent hum from a clearing just ahead. Pushing through the giant ferns, they saw it. The U.S.S. Sagittarius. The broad saucer had dug itself into the earth at the end of a long furrow, tilted at a dangerous angle, its hull scarred and smoking. Behind it, the two arms that reached back from the saucer's trailing edges had flexed and held, and the slim engineering hull they carried hung clear of the torn ground between them. Above it all, the warp nacelles still rode high on their pylons over the broken trees, as if nothing had happened. Emergency lights flickered across the saucer, and one of the impulse engines was still firing.

"She held," Anya breathed, and there was something close to reverence in it. "A bigger ship would have folded in half on that slide. She was built to be thrown around."

It was a miracle the ship hadn't been completely destroyed. The transport, whoever initiated it, had saved them just moments before impact.

"The ship..." Anya, the Typhlosion, rumbled, her voice a deep baritone. "The power conduits cannot be healthy. I need to get to engineering!"

"First, we need to understand what happened to us," T'Vella, the Gardevoir, stated, her voice trembling slightly. "This is... not logical."

"Forget logic! What are we?" Kenji, the Starly, chirped, flitting nervously from branch to branch, struggling to control his flight, almost tumbling a few times though seeming to get the jist of it.

Before anyone could answer, the world seemed to hold its breath. The chirping of unseen insects ceased. The gentle breeze died. The dappled sunlight filtering through the canopy vanished as if a switch had been thrown, plunging the forest into a sudden, deep twilight. A shadow fell over them, vast and swift, accompanied by a precipitous drop in temperature that made their fur and feathers stand on end. It was a pressure, a palpable, ancient authority that made their blood run cold. They looked up, and a collective gasp—a mixture of growls, chirps, and chimes—filled the air.

A colossal, serpentine creature, green as jade and impossibly long, descended from the heavens. It moved with a terrifying grace, its body coiling and uncoiling through the air. It had glowing yellow patterns running down its length and two large, fin-like appendages on its head. Its roar was a physical force, shaking the very ground they stood on. It fixed its burning, intelligent eyes on the wounded Starship, seeing not a vessel in distress, but an invader, a metal blight upon its sacred world.

"Tactical analysis, Zax!" Eva, the Absol, commanded, her voice a sharp bark that cut through the fear.

The Lucario, Zax, instinctively dropped into a combat stance, eyes wide as he took a defensive step back. "I have no data, Captain! It's! I- I don't know!" He caught himself, and the training came back through the panic. "Treeline. Now. Whatever that is, it's looking at the ship, not at us."

"There are a hundred and thirty-five people on that ship," Eva said. She didn't move.

The great serpent, Rayquaza, opened its mouth, and a sphere of crackling energy began to form. It saw the strange metal beast and the misshapen creatures below it as a singular threat. It would protect its world, as it always had.

The six Starfleet officers, ripped from their reality and thrust into new, terrifying forms, could only watch as the god-like creature prepared to unleash its fury upon their crippled ship. Their mission of exploration was over. A new, desperate mission had just begun: survival.

The sphere of energy in Rayquaza's mouth pulsed with an intensity that warped the air around it. It was a miniature star, a contained storm of pure power. With a deafening roar that was both sound and psychic assault, the creature unleashed the attack. A beam of golden energy, thick as a starship's nacelle, lanced across the sky toward the Sagittarius.

For a fraction of a second, the ship's damaged systems tried to respond. The navigational shielding, the only defense still partially active, flared to life, a shimmering, translucent bubble of energy enveloping the saucer section. The golden beam struck the shield, and for one glorious, defiant moment, it held. The air crackled, and the shield glowed white-hot under the strain, the hum of its emitters rising to a piercing scream. But it was like trying to stop a tidal wave with a pane of glass. The shield buckled, spiderweb cracks of pure energy spreading across its surface before it shattered into a billion glittering motes of light.

With the shield gone, the beam slammed into the Sagittarius's saucer section unimpeded. The ship, already crippled, screamed in protest. Metal groaned and tore as the beam carved a molten trench across the dorsal hull, vaporizing plating and blowing out entire decks. At the saucer's trailing edge, the one impulse engine that had kept burning through the crash guttered, flared orange, and sputtered out, its glow fading from the cowling like a coal going cold. The emergency lights flickered violently, then half of them went dark for good. The low hum of failing systems was replaced by the shriek of tortured metal and the crackle of exposed, arcing power conduits.

On the ground, the crew was thrown back by the sheer force of the impact. Eva was thrown onto her side, her mind still reeling from the sudden transformation. As she scrambled to her paws, her new, sharp senses were flooded with the dying screams of her ship. She could feel the catastrophic chain reaction of explosions—the two transporter rooms in the saucer section detonating from power overloads, the hydro-deck shattering and flash-boiling evident by the steam rising from where it should have been, entire corridors venting into the alien atmosphere as critical conduits failed. She knew, with a sickening certainty, that dozens of her crew had just been extinguished. "No!" she screamed, a raw, helpless cry of anguish that was lost in the cacophony of destruction.

She was running before she had decided to. Four bounding strides toward the clearing, and then the heat hit her like a wall, the air itself cooking, and she skidded to a stop with her eyes streaming. "Anya! The lifeboat deck, can they reach it?"

"The pod bays are on the ventral saucer!" Anya roared back. "If anyone's alive down there they'll be going for them! The hatches seal from inside, Captain, we can't get to them, there's no way in!"

"Then why aren't the pods launching?"

Nobody had an answer.

Rayquaza had not finished. It did not gather a sphere this time. It came around low over the ship's spine, so close that its passing flattened the treetops, turned its head, and dragged a thinner lash of the same molten gold along the top of the saucer from bow to stern, the way a hand might draw a blade along a table. Where it passed, the hull lit like a fuse.

"It's tearing us apart!" Anya, the Typhlosion, roared in anguish, the flames on her neck flaring with her distress. She watched as some of the long, dark strips of the dorsal phaser arrays glowed cherry-red for a heartbeat before they exploded outwards in a shower of molten metal. "The primary phaser arrays are gone!" A massive section of the hull plating above the crew quarters buckled and peeled away like a scab, revealing the decks within for a horrifying instant before a secondary explosion blew the entire section into the air. "Hull integrity on decks one through five is... it's gone!" Her engineer's mind could visualize the destruction with horrifying clarity—the catastrophic power failures cascading from the hit, the environmental seals on those decks failing in sequence, the life support systems winking out one by one. She wasn't just seeing a ship being destroyed; she was watching her life's work, and the lives of her friends, being unmade system by system.

"That wasn't a standard energy weapon," Zax, the Lucario, said, his voice tight. He had put himself between the Captain and the clearing without seeming to notice he had done it, one black paw pressed to the side of his head. "Disruptors, phasers... they overwhelm shields with brute force. This... This just ignored them. I've never seen anything like it. Even at full power, our defenses would have been useless…"

Helplessness. It was a foreign, bitter taste. They were Starfleet officers, trained to confront the unknown, to analyze, strategize, and overcome. But what could they do? They were small, fleshy creatures standing in a forest, watching a deity dismantle their vessel with casual, overwhelming force. Kenji, the Starly, threw himself into the air toward the ship, a pilot going for his post, and the heat and the chaos of the wind tumbled him straight back into the ferns, where he lay with his head under a wing, chirping in pure terror. Elara, the Kirlia, sank to the grass, overwhelmed by the wave of raw power and the collective despair of her crewmates. "They're still in there," she said, her voice thin. She had run the duty roster that morning. She knew every name on it. "Captain, they're still in there."

"I know," Eva said, and her voice shook on it. "I know every one of them."

T'Vella, the Gardevoir, stood unnaturally still, her new form rigid. Her eyes were fixed on the creature in the sky. It had opened its mouth again, and a point of brilliant light was coalescing before it, drawing in ambient energy and growing with a terrifying speed. "Captain," she said, her melodic voice flat with shock. "The creature is gathering energy into a focused point. It is preparing to fire again!"

As if on cue, Rayquaza let out another roar of fury and launched a third blast. This one struck the port nacelle. The pylon that carried it up from the engineering hull, designed to withstand the stresses of warp travel and the vacuum of space, snapped like a twig. The nacelle, with its Bussard collectors cold and dark, was torn from the ship and sent slamming into the forest beside it, where it exploded in a shower of sparks and duranium fragments. The Sagittarius listed heavily, the weight hanging from the two arms suddenly uneven, its frame groaning under the strain.

"It's targeting our propulsion," Zax stated, the observation grimly obvious. "It's systematically disabling us."

"Disabling? It's executing us," Eva snarled, her Absol form pacing back and forth, the sickle-like horn on her head catching the light. She looked at her crew—her brilliant, capable officers, now reduced to frightened animals—and then at the dying starship that had been their home. There were no orders to give, no clever strategies to employ. They were simply witnesses to their own end.

Then something dropped from the underside of the saucer.

It was small and blunt and white, and it fell the way a pod falls from a ship that is sitting on the ground instead of in orbit: straight down, two decks' worth, to thump into the churned earth beneath the hull in a puff of dirt. It rocked once and lay still, its running lights blinking a patient, pointless green.

"Pod," Anya breathed. "Somebody made it to a pod."

"They have to cycle the hatch," Zax said. "Thirty seconds, if it has power."

Eva was already moving along the edge of the heat, looking for a way down to it, counting the seconds under her breath in Russian.

Rayquaza coiled in the sky, its golden eyes burning with righteous anger. It saw the metal beast still twitching, still humming with a malevolent energy. It would not tolerate this intrusion. It gathered its power one last time, the energy sphere growing larger, brighter, more violent than before.

But the Sagittarius was not dead yet. Her bridge was empty; the six who should have been on it were standing in a forest, watching. Somewhere deeper inside the crippled ship, someone had been fighting a console for every second of this, working around a weapons lockout that would not lift without the bridge, routing through auxiliary control one dead relay at a time. It had taken them this long to get through. There was time for one volley. A single phaser array on the ventral saucer, the last with power behind it, woke and fired, a beam of brilliant orange energy, and a heartbeat later the forward tube spat a torpedo after it, glowing the same sullen orange: one of the Admiral's agony phaser torpedoes, fed by the same tuned power as the arrays.

The beam struck Rayquaza's side, and the great serpent hissed as its green scales were scorched black. Then the torpedo hit. It burst against the coils just below the scar, and the detonation did not stay where it landed: it crawled out across the scales in a sheet of crackling orange-red, and through them, into whatever a god had for nerves. The agonizer at its heart did exactly what it had been built to do.

Rayquaza screamed. It was not the roar of anger they had heard all afternoon. It was higher and rawer than that, and the whole length of the serpent convulsed, coils knotting and unknotting, its tail lashing a furrow through the treetops. For a moment it fell, dropping toward the forest like something cut loose, before it caught itself.

On the ground, Zax's lips drew back from his teeth in something that was not quite a smile. "Good," he said, very low.

Then the serpent hung there in the air, shuddering, and turned its head, and looked down at the ship. It had been angry before. What filled it now had no name in any language they knew. It let out a roar that shattered the very air, its eyes glowing with a terrifying, hateful intensity.

Anya's eyes widened in horror. She knew her ship. She knew every conduit, every power relay, every deck plate. And she knew exactly where the creature, now filled with vengeful rage, was aiming. "No..." she whispered, the sound a low rumble of dread. "No, not there..."

The final beam was a torrent of destruction. It didn't strike the outer hull. It pierced it, punching into the slim engineering hull slung beneath and behind the saucer, between the arms, where the warp core lived, with pinpoint, devastating accuracy. The same array fired once more, one last, desperate shot. The beam lanced out, a defiant orange needle against the sky, for less than a second before it sputtered and died, the power from its conduits severed. The streak of heated atmosphere it left behind faded slowly, a final, dying breath. For a moment, there was an unnatural silence. Most of the emergency lights on the Sagittarius died. A scatter of them stayed lit along the broken saucer, and somewhere along the hull a single alarm went on wailing, alone. The groaning of metal ceased.

Then, a new sound began. It was a low, deep thrumming from the heart of the ship, a vibration that traveled through the very ground they stood on. It grew rapidly in pitch and volume, becoming a piercing, world-ending scream of unrestrained power. A brilliant, blue-white light began to shine from the wound in the ship's hull—the same impossible light that had brought them to this world, now unleashed and uncontrolled. It was a light so pure it bleached all color from the world, casting their own monstrous shadows in stark, terrifying relief.

Under the saucer, a seam of light appeared along the engineering hull's hatch, a hand's width, grinding wider.

"The warp core!" Anya screamed, her voice a mixture of terror and grief, turning around in an attempt to get behind the crash site crater, as if that would save her. "It's breached! The containment's gone! Get down!"

Eva didn't get down. She was watching the hatch. It was Zax who hit her, a full-body tackle that drove them both into the dirt, his arms locked around her and his back to the light.

There was no time. The heart of the U.S.S. Sagittarius, its matter-antimatter reaction chamber, detonated. A wave of pure, unrestrained energy erupted outwards. It was not an explosion of fire and shrapnel, but one of silent, perfect annihilation. The engineering hull, the arms, the pylons and the rear half of the saucer didn't explode; they simply ceased to exist, their atoms unwritten from the fabric of reality in a silent, expanding sphere of blue-white nothingness. For a fraction of a second, the universe was silent. Then the shockwave hit. It was a physical wall of force, a concussive blast that flattened the forest for miles, the sound of a thousand ancient trees snapping like twigs arriving a heartbeat later.

The six officers were lifted from their feet, not by a force that pushed, but by one that unmade. In that final, silent instant, Eva's mind, her very soul, was brushed by the psychic echo of 135 lives being extinguished at once. It was a chorus of sudden, silent screams, a wave of pure terror and surprise that washed over her before vanishing into the void. She felt a searing heat that had nothing to do with fire, a pressure that squeezed the very air from her lungs, and saw that final, annihilating blue-white light burn itself onto the inside of her eyelids before the world dissolved into a merciful blackness. Their last connection to home, to everything they had ever known, was gone—not just gone, but unmade, reduced to its constituent energy in a flash of impossible power. They were truly and utterly alone, adrift in the ashes of their crew.
