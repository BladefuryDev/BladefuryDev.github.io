A high-pitched, incessant ringing was the first thing to pierce the silent blackness. It was the shriek of overloaded auditory nerves, the ghost of the explosion still echoing in Eva's skull. The first breath she took was a shallow, ragged gasp that tasted of ozone, shattered pine, and superheated metal. Her entire body was a single, resonant ache from the shockwave that had tossed her like a leaf in a hurricane.

There was a weight across her back. Warm, heavy, breathing.

"Zax," she croaked. The weight shifted, and a pained grunt answered her. He rolled off, and she heard him land on his side in the dirt and lie there a moment before he moved again. He had kept his back to the light. She would remember that.

She forced herself to push up, her new Absol body stiff and protesting, each movement sending a fresh wave of pain through her. With a deep, shuddering gasp, she finally drew a full breath, the ringing in her ears subsiding to a dull hum. As her vision cleared, she saw that the world was bathed in the soft, melancholy hues of sunset. Oranges, purples, and deep reds painted the clouds, casting long, distorted shadows across a scene of utter devastation.

The forest was gone. In its place was a flattened wasteland of splintered, smoldering wood and scorched earth, the trees laid down in rings around a single point like iron filings around a magnet. At that point, where the Sagittarius had rested, was a massive crater lined with black glass.

And in the crater, the ship was still standing.

Half of it. The forward half of the saucer sat on the glass where it had come to rest, scorched black, dark from end to end, its bow still buried in the far wall of the furrow it had dug on landing. Behind it there was nothing at all. No engineering hull, no arms, no pylons, no stern. The saucer simply ended, along a curve so clean it looked drawn, a mirror-bright arc of sheared metal where the sphere of annihilation had passed through it and taken everything on the other side. The cut ran through decks, through bulkheads, through the hollow of a turbolift shaft, and the evening light fell into the open rooms the way it falls into a dollhouse.

A soft chime, laced with pain, came from nearby. T'Vella, the Gardevoir, was slowly rising, her graceful form covered in soot and dirt. "The... the energy release..." she began, her logical mind struggling to quantify the catastrophe. "It was... total."

"Not total." Anya, the Typhlosion, was already on her feet, swaying, the flames on her neck a low, mournful flicker. She was staring at the wreck, and her voice did not sound like grief yet. It sounded like an engineer reading a gauge she did not believe. "Look at her. Look at the edge. Everything inside the sphere is gone, fine, that's physics, nothing stops that. But everything *outside* it is still there. That shockwave put a forest on the ground for a kilometer in every direction and she's still standing on her own hull. With the integrity field down since the crash." She shook her head slowly. "There should be nothing. There should be a hole. She held."

"She held," Eva agreed quietly, because somebody should say it twice.

One by one, the others stirred. Zax, the Lucario, pushed himself up to one knee, then to his feet, moving as if he had been through a hull breach drill with the gravity set too high. Elara, the Kirlia, was helping Kenji, the Starly, who was tangled in the roots of an upturned tree, chirping weakly. They were bruised, battered, and shaken to their very core, but they were alive. Their new, strange bodies had somehow absorbed the punishment better than their human forms ever could have.

"Report," Eva said. It came out as a growl, but it came out.

"Functional, Captain," Zax grunted. He looked at the crater, then at his own paws, then back at the crater. "But how? No one survives a core breach at this range. The shockwave alone should have atomized us. The radiation should have cooked us from the inside out."

"He is correct," T'Vella stated, her voice regaining a fraction of its usual composure. "The probability of organic matter surviving such an event is statistically zero. Our survival is a logical impossibility."

"It's not impossible, it's *wrong*," Anya said. "That wasn't a normal explosion. It was an unmaking. We shouldn't be here." She looked around at the fallen trees. "They didn't make it either, and they were further away than us."

Eva listened to them, their shared disbelief, and let them have it for exactly as long as it took her to find her feet properly. They had no answers. They had the undeniable fact of their own continued existence, and a ship that was still, barely, a ship.

Her eyes went to the underside of the saucer, to the place below the pod bays where the pod had come down. The glass there was scored and littered, and she could not make out what with, from here.

She counted to three in Russian, and then she put it away.

"The wreck is hot," she said. "The crater is hot. Zax, how long do we have before the radiation becomes a problem?"

Zax was silent for a moment, doing the sum. "The core is gone, so the worst of it went with it. But the theta residue from the reaction mass will cook off the wreckage as it cools. It's climbing now and it'll keep climbing for hours. Minutes of exposure are survivable. An hour isn't." He looked at the saucer. "Less inside."

"There might be something," Anya said. Her voice had found the grief now, thick with it. She glanced up, at the empty air above the cut where the mission pod had sat between the nacelles, and did not say the word shuttle. "An emergency locker. Supplies. Anything. If we walk away from her with nothing, Captain, we have nothing."

Eva looked at her crew. Six of them, standing on the edge of a crater in bodies they did not know, with no water, no food, no shelter and a night coming on. A captain's first duty was to the living.

"Then we go now, we go fast, and we take what we can carry," she said. "Nobody wanders. Nobody goes looking for anyone. Anya, you know the deck plan better than I do, you lead. Zax, rear. Fifteen minutes and we are out of there, whatever we've found. Understood?"

A ragged chorus of agreement. Anya was already moving, picking her way over the lip of the crater, and the rest of them fell in behind her in the order Eva had given, down the long slope into the grave of their ship.

The slope changed under them as they went. Scorched dirt gave way to a brittle, dark crust where the earth had fused, and the crust gave way to glass, and the glass was hot. The heat came up through it. Within a few meters Eva was lifting her paws between steps, and she could hear Kenji's hiss of pain as his talons found the surface and left it again. A wave of warmth, like standing before an open furnace, carried the sharp, metallic tang of ozone and the sickeningly sweet smell of vaporized duranium. Ahead, the ruin of the saucer hissed and popped, a symphony of destruction in its final, cooling throes. The groans of stressed, contracting metal were punctuated by the sharp tinkle of cooling glass and the soft, final sighs of dying systems. To Anya, every sound was a dagger in her engineer's heart.

"Uninhabitable," Zax said, his voice stripped of everything but the fact. "Don't touch bare metal. Don't breathe deep."

The litter on the glass began before they reached the hull. Small things at first, scattered in a fan across the slope: a length of conduit, a strip of seat webbing, something that might once have been a handle. Eva stepped around a curved shard of white composite the size of a dinner plate and did not let herself think about what it was. Then Anya stopped.

At her feet lay a flat rectangle of grey alloy, one edge sheared clean, the other three bent, lying face-up on the glass as if it had been placed there. The stencilled lettering on it was still legible. **POD 04 - RACK B - DO NOT OBSTRUCT.**

"Computer rack," Anya said, very quietly. "Side plate. It sits beside the nav computer, inside the pod, about there." She touched her own ribs. "You'd have your shoulder against it."

Nobody asked which pod. There had only been one. Eva looked back along the fan of debris and understood what she was looking at, and saw the others understand it: the curved shard, the webbing, the handle. The hatch had never finished opening. It would not have mattered if it had. Minimum safe distance from a core breach was measured in kilometers, and the pod had been lying under the ship with its running lights on.

"The edge of the sphere went through the bay," Zax said. "What the sphere didn't take, the shockwave did."

Anya bent, and picked up the plate, and turned it over in her paws, and put it down again exactly where it had been.

"Keep moving," Eva said. Her voice was steady. She did not look down again until they reached the hull.

They found a way in near the bow: the forward observation lounge, whose massive viewport had been shattered, the duranium frame twisted and curled inwards. Eva had taken her coffee at that window every morning for two years. They climbed through it, their paws crunching on the broken glass, and were inside.

The lounge was black, and the ship was not silent.

Somewhere aft, deep in the hull, a single alarm was still sounding. Not the full-throated chorus of a starship in distress; one speaker, alone, giving the red alert klaxon over and over into the dark, the long two-tone swell that every one of them had heard a thousand times and had never once heard without a bridge crew moving under it. Rise, fall. Rise, fall. It had been sounding when they woke in the ferns, before Rayquaza, before any of it, and it had not stopped. A red alert strobe pulsed somewhere down a side corridor in time with nothing, lighting the dust and letting it go, lighting it and letting it go.

"That's not the grid," Anya said. Her voice was very even. "The grid's gone. Those are the local capacitors, one per fixture, wired straight to the light or the speaker so an alert survives a power drop. They hold whatever charge they had when the ship died, and they'll run the last command they got until they're empty." She listened to the klaxon swell and fall. "That one's got a few hours in it."

Every surface was coated in a fine, grey dust, thick as fresh snowfall, that rose in slow clouds where they stepped and settled on their fur. Anya ran a claw through the dust on a twisted console, and her expression turned from grief to horror.

"This dust..." she whispered, her voice cracking. "The heat from the blast... it would have flash-cremated anyone who was still inside. This is... this is them."

A heavy, sacred silence fell over the team. Zax stood at attention, his new ears flattening in the way his antennae would once have drooped, an Andorian gesture in a Lucario body. T'Vella closed her eyes, processing the logical, yet emotionally devastating, truth. Elara made a small sound and pressed both hands over her mouth. She had run the duty roster that morning. She was standing in it.

"Elara," Eva said. "With me. Stay close."

Anya went to the wall first. Every corridor and lounge on the ship had them: a survival locker set into the panelling at floor level, marked with a stripe, stocked for exactly this. She found the stripe by feel under the dust, got her claws into the seam, and pulled, and the panel came away in two pieces. Behind it the locker was a slumped shape of metal, melted and resolidified into the cavity that held it, and what had been inside it was a single black mass she did not try to identify. The heat that had made the dust had been through here first.

"Too close to the window," she said. "Everything this side of the hull is cooked. We go in."

They moved.

The corridors beyond the lounge were a nightmare of mangled passageways and collapsed decks, and the klaxon went with them, nearer in some passages and farther in others, a second speaker somewhere above joining it a half-beat out of step, so that the two swells chased each other through the hull and never met. the familiar layout now a trap of exposed wiring and sharp, torn bulkheads. Anya led them aft and down, toward the crew quarters and the emergency stores, and the ship went by on either side of them in pieces. The mess hall doors, jammed half open on a room full of overturned chairs. A turbolift, its car gone, the shaft a black throat they skirted on a ledge of buckled plating. A corridor junction where Kenji stopped dead and said, in a very small voice, "That's the way to the flight deck." The passage he was looking down ran aft toward the lift that climbed to the mission pod, and it ran about twenty meters, and then it ended in a bright, clean edge and the evening sky. Nobody told him to keep moving, because he already had.

Anya tried the next locker thirty meters in, in a corridor with its ceiling intact. The panel came off whole this time, and for a second that felt like something. Then the box behind it: the same slump of metal, the same black mass, a ration tube fused to the lid like a dead thing. She stared at it longer than she had at the first.

"The heat came through the decks, not just the hull," she said. "It's inside the walls. We need somewhere it didn't reach." She looked aft and down, toward the crew deck, toward the parts of the ship furthest from the window and the trench. "Deeper."

"Deeper is more radiation," Zax said.

"Deeper is the only place anything's left."

Near the strobe, a wall panel had cracked along its seam and sagged open, and Anya slowed as she passed it the way she might have slowed at a familiar face. Behind it, where the trunk line should have run, the conduit was a black, glassy ribbon, fused end to end. Beside it, intact and faintly warm, sat the fixture's own capacitor, a cell no bigger than her paw, and from it two thin leads ran to the strobe and on to the two dead panels they had just walked past. The whole of what this wall knew about the ship was those two panels. It was showing her what she already knew, and she looked anyway. "Yeah," she said to it, quietly. "I see you."

A little further on, the deck changed under their paws. The fire suppression had gone off here, every nozzle in the ceiling spent, the residue lying over the corridor in a dull, chalky skin, and Eva's first thought was that it had been useless, a system doing its last job for no one. Then she noticed that she had stopped lifting her paws between steps. The deck was cool. Whatever had burned through the corridors around this one had not burned here. It had not been for no one.

The ship was not finished dying around them. In the dark, an EPS conduit split along its length and spat a fan of blue-white sparks across the passage, and they went past it one at a time with their fur standing up, the smell of burning insulation sharp in their noses. The deck under them was warm. Somewhere below, something large let go with a groan and a long, grinding slide, and the whole corridor dropped a hand's width and stopped, and the dust came down from the ceiling in a soft grey curtain. Far behind them, the red strobe lit the falling dust once more, and went out, and did not come back.

"She's settling," Anya said. "The frame's taking loads it was never meant to take, without the field. We don't have fifteen minutes. We have less."

They heard the next one before they felt it. A crack, from behind them, back toward the bow. Then a roar of collapsing structure, and a wall of dust rolled up the corridor and broke over them, and when it cleared the way they had come in was gone, a solid wedge of deck plating and ceiling filling the passage floor to ceiling.

For a moment nobody said anything.

"Forward's closed," Zax said. "We find another way out. Keep moving, Chief."

It was Anya who found it, and it was not a wall locker at all. Pinned under a collapsed deck plate at the end of a corridor on the crew deck was a scorched, half-melted but intact emergency survival locker, the heavier kind that rode inside every escape pod, built to survive a bad landing. The pod bay it belonged to had been three decks up. It had come down through the ship when the decks went, and it had stopped here.

Working together, the hulking Typhlosion and the powerful Lucario pried the wreckage away. Inside, they found a small cache of tools: a treasure beyond measure in their new reality.

Zax retrieved a sleek, black Type-1 phaser and turned it over in his paws. The grip was deformed along one side, the casing bubbled where the heat had got into it, and when he thumbed the charge indicator it lit in a sullen amber at the bottom of its range. He popped the cell housing. Of the three sarium-krellide cells stacked inside, two had slumped into each other and gone dull, and only the third, nearest the grip, still showed the clean green of a live charge. "One cell," he said, and seated the housing again with grim satisfaction. "A few shots on stun, if I'm careful." It was low, but it was something. T'Vella drew out the medical kit and opened it, and closed it again. The heat had been through it: the vials were glass dust and the hypospray had fused into a single lump of plastic and alloy. "Nothing," she said. "We leave without medicine." Anya's eyes lit up as she found what she was looking for: a standard tricorder, its casing and screen cracked but the device itself still functional. Amidst the scattered tools and ration packs, they also found a single, standard-issue survival satchel, its tough canvas scorched but intact. Anya opened it. Tucked securely inside, cushioned by the bag's padding, was the portable shelter emitter. It was bulky, but it had survived. This satchel, and the precious few items they could fit inside it, was all they had left.

"The rations may be useless," T'Vella observed, her Gardevoir eyes examining the ingredients listed on the side. "Our new biology may not be capable of processing them."

As if to prove her point, Kenji pecked curiously at a tube, then recoiled, chirping in disgust.

Anya had the tricorder open before the others had finished looking. She drew the scanner from its port with a paw that was not quite steady and held it up to the air. The device chirped, thought about it, and gave her a number, and the number went up while she watched.

"Zax was right," she said. "Theta's climbing as she cools. We're at the edge of what I'd call safe and it's going the wrong way." She snapped the scanner home. "Ten minutes, Captain. Then it starts costing us."

"Then we go. Which way?"

Anya turned in a slow circle, reading the ship she knew by heart against the ruin in front of her. "Port side. Deck four. The first beam opened the hull above the quarters, there'll be a way out through the trench."

"Then that's the way." Eva did not move yet. She looked down at the satchel at Anya's feet, at the meager collection of tools they were about to carry out of here. A tricorder, a near-empty phaser, a shelter, rations they probably could not eat. It was so little to face a hostile world with. But it was more than nothing, and it was all the Sagittarius had left to give them, and she would not take it without a word.

She looked at the last remnants of her crew, her family, their strange new forms standing in the dust of everyone else. She saw T'Vella, the brilliant Vulcan scientist, now a graceful, almost fragile-looking being. She saw Zax, her stoic, disciplined tactical officer, now a creature of raw, canine strength. She saw Anya, the cheerful, genius engineer, transformed into a hulking beast with a mantle of living fire. And she saw Elara and Kenji, her capable operations and helm officers, now a small, trembling Kirlia and a tiny, frightened Starly.

One hundred and forty-one souls had been aboard the Sagittarius. Six were standing in this corridor. The weight of that loss, of her command, settled on her with a crushing finality, and she let it, for one breath, because she would not have the luxury again.

"Listen to me," she said, and her voice found the authority of her command and held it. "The Sagittarius is gone. Our mission is over." She let the words hang in the air, heavy and final. "Our commission is over. Our training isn't. Look at what we survived. We are still a crew. T'Vella, you are still my science officer. Zax, tactical. Anya, engineering. Elara, operations. Kenji, you are still my eyes in the sky. And I am still your Captain."

She met each of their gazes, her own Absol eyes burning with a fierce, new resolve. "Our new mission is simple: we survive. Together. We learn, we adapt, and we overcome. That is what Starfleet taught us to do." Anya shouldered the satchel. "Let's move out."

Anya took them port and up, through corridors that grew hotter and brighter as they climbed toward the trench, and the klaxon came with them the whole way. They left it still sounding. Eva heard it rise and fall, rise and fall, the last order the Sagittarius would ever give, to a crew that had already answered it.

The trench on deck four was exactly where Anya had said it would be: a long, ragged wound where the first beam had peeled the hull open above the crew quarters, and the evening sky showed through it, deep red going to violet. They climbed. Zax went first, then reached back for each of them in turn, and Eva came last, because that was where the captain went.

They stood for a moment on the hull, on the glassy, heated slope of the crater, the ruin of their past at their backs. From up here the cut was close enough to touch, the mirror-bright edge of the saucer a few meters aft, and beyond it the clean, empty glass where the rest of the ship had been. Eva did not look at it. She looked at the faces of her crew, their strange new forms lit by the last rays of an alien sun.

Then she turned her back on the wreckage for good and began the climb out of the crater.

One by one, her crew followed, each lost in their own private grief. Zax's mind was already treating the wilderness as hostile territory, his senses on high alert, his grief channeled into a cold, tactical vigilance. Anya could not bring herself to look back; the image of her ship, her beautiful, tough little ship, standing on her own hull with half of herself gone, was too much to bear. T'Vella walked with a stoic grace, her mind a whirlwind of variables that did not add up, the loss of her ship and crew a problem with no solution. Elara trembled, the silence of the flattened forest, with no voices in it but theirs, a void that terrified her more than any explosion. Kenji, small and frightened, simply followed, his world reduced to the comforting presence of his crewmates. Eva led them, her face a mask of iron resolve, but inside, her heart was a singularity of loss. Each step away from the crater felt like a betrayal of those they had left behind.

As the first stars began to appear in the unfamiliar sky, the six of them, carrying the last remnants of their old lives, walked into the deep shadows of the shattered wilderness, and a strange and terrifying world closed around them.

…

Beyond the hills to the east, a town had been watching the sky all afternoon.

They had seen the silver thing fall. They had seen Rayquaza's long shadow pass over the hills and heard its roar come rolling in across the fields, and the whole town had gone very still, the way a town does when a god is angry somewhere nearby. In the great tree at its heart, the ones whose job it was to go toward trouble stood on the balconies and waited for an order no one wanted to give.

Then, at sunset, the light. A pillar of blue-white that stood up out of the western forest and turned the evening into noon for one silent second, and went out. The sound reached them a long count later, a single flat crack that shook dust from the tree's roots and set every bird in town into the air at once.

Deep beneath the tree, in a room that few in the town had ever seen, someone had been sitting very still for an hour, feeling the thing that had fallen the way she felt everything that touched the world: as a presence, enormous and strange and full of minds. When the light came, she felt a hundred and thirty-five of them go out at once, a chorus cut off mid-note, and she put her hand over her mouth and did not move for a long time.

Then, slowly, she lowered it. Because in the silence where the chorus had been, faint and far away and walking east through the dark, there were six.
