They had gone barely ten paces from the Guild's doors when a squawk went up behind them. "Team Starfall! Wait! Wait, wait, wait!"

Chirp came flapping out after them with a scroll in one claw and a small cloth pouch in the other, and landed in their path with the air of someone who had chased a great many new teams out of a great many doors and had never once enjoyed it. "New-team stipend," he announced, and held the pouch out to Eva. It jingled. Then he looked down at her four paws, and at the pouch, and back at her, and held it out to Anya instead. "To get you started. Supplies, food, anything useful. Spend it wisely."

"Thank you," Eva said.

"Don't thank me. Sign for it." He unrolled the scroll with a snap and held out a now-familiar cut-stick pencil. "Mark here."

Eva looked at the scroll. It was not the flowing script of the ceiling maps but the little footprint pictographs of last night's registration form, and it meant exactly as much to her as either. Beside her, T'Vella had already reached for the pencil, and Eva gave the smallest shake of her head. The Guildmaster had asked them to read their writing back to him. Chirp would ask a great deal more than that, and a great deal louder.

She lifted a forepaw instead and pressed it to the scroll beside the line his claw was tapping. It left a neat, slightly dusty print.

Chirp looked at the print. He looked at her. He looked at the print again, and his head tilted, slowly, the whole way over, the way he might have eyed a beetle he did not entirely trust. There was nothing wrong with it. Half the Pokémon in this town had paws. "Hm," said Chirp. He rolled the scroll up, tucked it under his wing, and flapped back inside without another word, which somehow felt worse than anything he could have said.

"He didn't argue," Kenji said from Zax's shoulder, once the doors had swung shut behind him. He sounded almost disappointed. "Does that mean it counts? You just signed for money with your foot, Captain."

"Forepaw," said Eva.

"Chirp would have objected," T'Vella said. She was still looking at the doors, as if she could see the scroll through them. "Loudly, and with a rule to cite. He did not. Given everything we have seen of him, I consider his silence conclusive." She paused. "What interests me is what it said."

Anya weighed the pouch in her paw. "It said 'Eva.' Probably."

"Not necessarily. Several of the characters on that scroll were prints, and they were not all the same print. I counted at least four kinds. One of them was a bird's foot, not unlike Chirp's." T'Vella's head tilted, very slightly, in a way that was nothing at all like Chirp's. "If each print stands for its species, the Captain has signed 'Absol.' If the system is phonetic, she may have signed a single syllable. If it is neither, one Absol forepaw could mean almost anything."

"Like what?" said Kenji.

"I do not know. That is what interests me."

Kenji thought about it. "What if it means 'volunteers for chores'?"

Zax made a small sound that, from anyone else, might have been a laugh. Anya shook the pouch until it jingled. "Whatever it said, it bought us this. It's a start."

"Market first," said Eva. "Then east."

The market filled an open plaza near the heart of Havenwood, loud and bright in the morning sun. Stalls of woven reed stood in a loose ring, and the biggest of them belonged to two Kecleon, one green and one purple. Their goods lay spread on broad leaves and flat stones: berries stacked in neat pyramids, seeds of every shape, coils of rope, little glass orbs that caught the light. Under a cloth awning at the back, well out of reach, sat a single tray of gnarled seeds that glowed a faint gold.

Heads turned as they crossed the square. Most turned back again, the way heads turn for any new team, but not all of them. A Furret at the bread stall drew her kit in against her side until Eva had gone past, and an old Sudowoodo watched her all the way across without once blinking. Nobody said anything this time. Eva kept her eyes on the Kecleon's stall and her tail still, and let them look.

"Welcome, welcome!" The green Kecleon's eyes swiveled toward them one at a time, the left settling on Eva and the right on the coin pouch in Anya's paw. "A new team! You've got the look. Everyone has the look on their first morning. What'll it be? Rope, seeds, berries for the road? Orbs, if you're feeling rich." His right eye weighed the pouch. "You're not feeling rich."

"We are not," T'Vella agreed.

The purple Kecleon said nothing at all. He sat at the far end of the stall with his tail curled around a post and watched them, and he went on watching them the whole time his brother talked.

Eva looked over the stall and priced nothing, because the little slates propped against every pile were marked in the same footprint pictographs as Chirp's scroll. "A bag," she said. "Something sturdy. Nothing fancy." As the green Kecleon turned to rummage behind the counter, she lowered her voice and nodded at the scorched Starfleet satchel slung at Anya's shoulder. "That stands out too much. We need to look like a normal team, not survivors."

Anya unslung it without argument. The Kecleon came back with a plain leather satchel, well stitched, with a wide flap and a strap long enough for a Typhlosion. While Eva paid by pushing coins across the counter until he nodded, Anya turned her back to the street and moved their few belongings over: the tricorder and the phaser first, quickly, and then the last handful of blue berries from the forest.

The green Kecleon's eyes lit up at the sight of them. "Oran Berries! Picked wild, were they? Good ones, too, nice and firm."

So that was what they were called. Anya rolled the old satchel up tight and tucked it into the bottom of the new one, the last piece of the *Sagittarius* they carried gone out of sight. Eva bought six more Orans, and a few of the small pink fruit stacked beside them.

"Pecha Berries," the Kecleon said, wrapping them in a leaf. "Cures poison. Every team should carry a couple. You never know with Venonat."

"Pecha," Kenji repeated from Zax's shoulder, pleased with himself. "Like the café wanted. Six of them." He leaned out over the counter until Zax put up a paw to stop him from falling off. "What are the glowing ones?"

"Ah." The green Kecleon's voice dropped into something close to reverence. "Reviver Seeds. Fresh in this morning. One of those brings a fainted Pokémon straight back up, good as new."

"The Guildmaster told everyone to stock up on those," said Kenji.

"And bless him for it." The Kecleon beamed. "Best salesman I've got, and I don't even pay him."

"Back from what, exactly?" asked Anya.

"From fainted." He looked at her as if she had asked which way was up. "What else?"

T'Vella studied the tray with polite suspicion. "A seed that restores a collapsed patient to full function," she said. "A merchant's claims are rarely calibrated."

"Then don't buy one," the Kecleon said cheerfully. "You can't afford one anyway." He named a price, and Eva did not need to read to know it was several times everything in their pouch. She put away the coins they had left. Behind his brother, the purple Kecleon reached out one long claw and slid a single coin back across the counter toward her. He still did not say anything.

The road east left Havenwood by the eastern gate. For its first stretch it kept the town's crystal posts, one every few hundred paces, amber even in daylight, and Anya stopped at the first of them with the tricorder held out at arm's length. The power bar on the cracked screen flickered. In the log, a thin line of input opened on the same frequency as the crystals in their room.

"It works out here too," she said, delighted. "Slow, but it works."

"How slow?" Eva asked.

Anya did the arithmetic with her lips moving. "If I stood right here all day, maybe one more segment by nightfall. If I could get it to a quarter, I could run a proper diagnostic. See what's still alive in there besides the scanner."

"We are not stopping at every lamppost," said Zax.

"I didn't ask to." She caught up with them at the next post anyway, and the one after that, until the posts gave out where the road became a dirt trail and she put the tricorder away with a sigh.

The trail wound on through open fields, sun-dappled and humming with insects. Cinder's team had been out this way ahead of them: the long grass was trodden flat along the edges, and here and there a cut branch had been thrown to one side. The Guildmaster had promised no threats on the way, and there were none. A pair of Pidgey rose from the grass ahead of them and settled again further on. Off to the left, a Bidoof was building something in a ditch and did not look up.

Zax fell into step beside Eva, as far out of the others' earshot as six people on one trail could manage. "The phaser," he said. "I want it on me."

"It stays in the satchel."

"Captain, if we are engaged, the draw time from that bag—"

"—is better than explaining it to everyone who sees it on your hip." Eva kept her eyes on the trail. "We're a normal team, Zax. Normal teams don't carry energy weapons. If the Guildmaster is testing anything today, it's whether we can follow an order like *observe only*." She glanced at him. "It's reconnaissance. We won't need it."

Zax walked a few paces in silence. "Noted," he said, in the tone that meant he had noted it, filed it, and fully expected to be proven right.

Elara had gone quiet at the back of the line. Since they had woken in these bodies there had been a faint layer of something under everything else, like a conversation heard through a bulkhead, and out here in the open it was clearer than it had ever been. "It's strange," she said softly. "The Pokémon in these fields. They feel calm. Content."

Zax glanced back at her, skeptical. "Quantify 'feel,' Coordinator."

"I can't." Elara frowned and touched her fingers to her temple. "It's a background noise. A sort of hum. Like a room where everyone is comfortable."

"It may be subconscious pattern recognition," T'Vella said. "Posture, movement, the absence of alarm calls." She was quiet for a step. "However, I have been registering something as well. A low, diffuse field, too faint to localize. Its strength appears to correlate with a lack of aggression among the local fauna." She considered her own words with some distaste. "It is, statistically, calming."

Zax did not answer that. He had felt it too, a quiet in the air that had nothing to do with sound, and he had been ignoring it since the gate because he had no word for it and did not care for things he had no word for.

"A new sensory input," Eva said. "Note it, all of you. We'll compare when we have more data. For now, stay sharp."

The easy walk ended where the fields did. Ahead, a line of dark, gnarled trees rose out of the grass like a wall, their branches knotted together overhead, and the air coming off them was cool and thick with the smell of wet earth and rot. Elara's hum went out like a light. Where it had been there was nothing, and the nothing was worse.

A wooden signpost leaned at the edge of the trees, half swallowed by moss. Several lines of footprint pictographs ran across it, and under them someone had drawn a cheerful little cartoon: a Pikachu tripping over a looping tree root, stars of pain bursting from its head.

Zax looked at it with the grim satisfaction of a man proven right. "A signpost," he said. "Which we cannot read."

"I can read *that* bit," said Kenji.

"The pictogram suggests hazardous terrain," T'Vella said. "Root structures across the path, specifically. The text may say more." She tilted her head. "Or it may say 'Watch your step.' We have no way to know which."

Someone had set one of the town's crystal posts beside the sign to mark the way in, and it was dark. Not dim: dark, a grey six-sided lump on a stick. Anya held the tricorder against its face and watched the log. No input. She tried the other side and the top, and got the same nothing each time. "It isn't burnt out," she said slowly. "It's empty. Like something drank it."

Eva studied the dark crystal a moment longer, then the trees. "Kenji. Aerial sweep. Stay above the canopy. Look for any unusual concentrations of Pokémon, any clearings. Report back in five."

Kenji chirped and shot off Zax's shoulder, climbed, and was gone above the leafy ceiling.

Above the canopy the morning came back all at once, bright and wide and blue, and he let himself climb a little higher than he needed to, just for the feel of it. He had landings down now. Everything else this body could do, he had barely touched, and five minutes alone in open sky was too good to waste.

He tipped one wing a hair to bank, and the world swung a quarter of the way around before he had finished deciding to turn. He wobbled, overcorrected, wobbled the other way. The *Sagittarius* had taken a long, patient breath of inertia to answer her helm. This body had no dampers and no lag at all. It answered before he did.

So he gave it less. A flick of the tail. A wing tucked by a feather's width, not half its length. He tried a snap roll and came out of it level, then cut a flat turn around a dead spike of branch so tight that the horizon pivoted around him like the view from a racing drone, and he laughed out loud into the wind. Twitchy. Twitchy and quick and *precise*, if he kept his focus. With practice he could put himself anywhere he liked.

Then he remembered he was supposed to be looking at something, and looked down.

From up here the Murky Wood was a dark green fist in the middle of the fields, and the fist was going grey from the inside: the treetops at its heart were the color of ash, dull and bare, spreading outward in a rough circle like a stain in cloth. Nothing flew over it. Nothing rose from it. Over the fields the warm air had lifted under his wings; over the wood it was cool and sinking, and nearer the grey it went flat and dead, so that he had to work for every wingbeat. He dropped and swung a tight circle around one treetop to look in at a nest. Empty. He made one wide pass along the grey edge and found he did not want to make a second.

Below, the rest of them waited at the edge of the trees. Nothing moved in there. No birds called. The breeze stirring the grass behind them did not seem to reach past the first row of trunks.

"I don't like this place," Zax muttered. The appendages at the back of his head had gone stiff. "The air in there is wrong."

Kenji was back in less than five. He dropped onto Zax's shoulder in a single clean landing and spoiled it at once with a nervous flutter. "It's quiet, Captain. Too quiet. Nests with nobody in them, nobody flying, nothing in the clearings. And the middle's gone grey. The tops of the trees, right in the center, like somebody painted over them. The air over it's dead, too. No lift at all." He fluffed himself up and smoothed himself down again. "It's like the whole forest is holding its breath."

Eva looked at the dark line of trunks a moment longer. Something about this place was wrong. She could not have said what. Not the dead post, not the silence, not anything Kenji had seen from the air, though all of it fit. It sat lower than reasoning, a cold prickle that started between her shoulders and lifted the fur all along her back, and this body seemed very sure that it wanted to be somewhere else. But they had a mission, and they had come to do it.

She let out a slow breath. "Alright," she said. "We're going in. Standard exploratory formation. Zax on point, Kenji with him. Anya and T'Vella in the middle. Elara, with me at the rear." Her voice dropped. "Observe only. Nobody touches anything they don't have to. Let's get this done quickly. If anyone sees something, say something."

The change was immediate. The temperature fell as though a door had shut behind them, and a thin mist lay over the ground and curled around their legs at every step. The branches overhead grew so thick that the morning sank to a grey half-light. And it was silent. A forest this size should have been loud at this hour, full of birdsong and insects and small things getting out of their way. This one was so quiet they could hear the mist dripping from the leaves.

They found the first nest within a hundred paces, a neat cup of woven grass wedged into the fork of a tree at Zax's eye level. It had been abandoned. The three eggs in it were cold, and one had cracked. A little further on, the trail passed a berry bush so heavy with ripe fruit that its branches bent to the ground, and Anya had reached toward it before Zax's paw closed on her wrist.

"A hungry forest," he said quietly, "and nobody has eaten these. Ask why first."

"Then let's ask." Anya fished the tricorder out of the satchel with her free paw. Zax did not let go of the other one. The cracked screen woke slowly, the power bar no fatter than it had been at the gate, and she held it over the bush and let it work. They were Orans, the same blue as the six in the satchel, ripe and whole, without a mark on them.

"Nothing wrong with them," she said, frowning. "No rot, no toxins, nothing growing on them that shouldn't be." She dug one of the market Orans out of the satchel, scanned it, then the bush again, and the frown deepened. "But they're quiet. Ours is still alive, the way fruit is alive: it breathes, it's ticking over. These are barely ticking. Like they've sat in cold storage for a month, except they're still on the branch."

"And the rest of us?" Eva asked.

Anya swept the tricorder across the team, one after another, and watched the readings. It took a moment. "There's something," she said at last. "A drag. Very small, right down at the edge of what this thing can see. I can't tell you what it is or where it's coming from. I'm not even sure it isn't the tricorder." She thumbed it off before it could eat any more of its charge. "But it's on all of us. And it wasn't there in the fields."

Kenji shifted on Zax's shoulder. "So nobody eats anything in here."

"Nobody eats anything in here," said Eva.

Anya took her paw back. Every so often they passed another of the crystal posts, leaning and half grown over, where someone had once marked a trail through the wood, and every one of them was dark. Up ahead, T'Vella had crouched by a patch of soft earth where the path dipped, studying a mess of prints pressed into the mud. "Several species," she said. "Small, medium, at least one large. The strides are long and the toes dug deep: they were running." She looked up along the line of them. "All of them away from the center of the wood. None coming back."

Zax was not looking at the ground. He was looking up. Three times his own height above the trail, the branches had been broken off clean, a whole row of them, with the raw wood showing pale where the bark had torn. A little further on, a long, scorched groove ran down one trunk and into the earth. "Whatever came through here did not walk," he said quietly. "It flies. And it is big."

Elara gasped. Both hands flew to her head and she staggered against Eva's side. "Something's afraid. Terrified. It came from in there." She pointed past T'Vella, into the grey. "It was only for a second, but it was so *loud*."

At the same moment, the tricorder in Anya's paws gave a shrill beep. She clapped her other paw over the speaker and stared at the screen. "Energy spike. Big one. Not biological, not geological, nothing I recognize. It flared and it's gone." She pointed, and her claw lined up with Elara's hand. "Same direction."

Two instruments, one event. Eva needed nothing more. "That's it. We have our data," she said, low and firm. "The Guildmaster's orders were clear. We pull back now and report."

They had turned for the path when the sound reached them. It came out of the mist somewhere close: a low, wet scraping, slow and rhythmic, like something heavy and broken being dragged across the forest floor, with a clicking under it that never quite stopped. The fog threw it around between the trunks. It could have been ahead of them or behind, and for all they could tell, it was between them and the way out. Every one of them dropped into a crouch without being told.

Zax held up a paw. "Hostile. Unidentified." His ears swung, trying to fix it, and failed. "I can't place it."

Elara flinched again. This time it was not a scream from the dark; it was smaller and stranger, and it came from right beside her. She shook her head hard, as if she had water in her ears.

"Elara," Eva said. "Report."

"I don't—" Elara pressed her fingers to her temples. "Right before someone speaks, I feel it coming. Not the words. Just a pressure, a jumble of noise and feeling, and it builds and builds, and then you talk and it goes." She looked at them helplessly. "It's every one of you. It's too much."

Zax gave her a long, strange look before the scraping pulled his eyes back to the mist.

Eva felt her next words gather, and saw Elara wince before she said them. "We fall back on a different vector," she said. "Circle around it and come out further along the edge."

"Captain, I don't think we should." Anya had not taken her eyes off the tricorder, which had begun a low, steady hum. "That signature is back, and it isn't flaring this time. It's constant. A localized field." She turned the screen toward Eva. "And I think it's what killed the posts. All of them, the whole way in. They're not dim, they're drained. Something is pulling the energy out of this wood."

T'Vella straightened, her red eyes gone distant as she followed it through. "Anya is correct. The Guildmaster tasked us with identifying the source of the fear. A field that drains energy from its surroundings is very likely part of that answer." She looked at Eva. "To report the symptom without its cause would be a partial report. It would be illogical to come this far and return with half of what we were sent for."

"Or we report what we have, alive." Zax did not take his eyes off the mist. "Unknown hostile, unknown position, and we are inside its territory. Our orders were reconnaissance only. Every step further in is a step we have to make again on the way out."

Eva weighed it. The officer in her agreed with him: observe, report, no heroics. But the order had been to find the source of the fear, and so far they had found only its edge. And whatever could drain a forest dry was half an hour's walk from a town full of crystal posts.

"We go on," she said. "Our objective is updated. We advance toward that reading, slowly, and we observe. We do not engage. Zax, take point. Kenji, stay low. Nobody makes a sound."

As they formed up, Elara stumbled. Five minds' worth of words-about-to-be-spoken pressed in on her at once, and her foot caught on a root. T'Vella reached out a slender arm to steady her. "Are you all right?"

The moment T'Vella's hand closed on her arm, the noise stopped. All of it. In its place came one clear, wordless thing, warm and steady and unmistakably not her own: concern. T'Vella's concern, plain as a voice on a clean channel. For the first time since the crash the static had resolved into a signal, and Elara stared up at the Vulcan with her eyes very wide.

T'Vella paused. Whatever she saw in Elara's face, she clearly meant to ask about it. But Eva was already signaling them forward, and T'Vella let go, and the static came rushing back. Shaken, Elara fell into line.

A few paces on, T'Vella dropped back beside her. "What did you perceive?" she asked, barely above a breath.

Elara hesitated. "You," she whispered. "You were worried about me."

T'Vella walked on for a moment without answering. "That is accurate," she said at last, and said nothing more, but she stayed at Elara's shoulder from then on.

Anya walked with the tricorder held low against her chest and her claws working. First she killed the hum: *nobody makes a sound*, Eva had said, and that went for the equipment too. Then she pulled the spike back up out of the log, the one that had flared and vanished when Elara cried out, and laid it across the cracked screen as a template. A shape to match against.

"Narrow band," she breathed, half to herself. "Keyed to that profile and nothing else. No bio, no geo, no ambient EM. Passive only." Every sensor she switched off was charge she got to keep, and the power bar needed all the help it could get. She centered the band on the spike's peak and opened it a little either side.

"Four percent tolerance," T'Vella murmured from behind her. "The spike was clean. Any wider and the background field will bleed into it."

Anya narrowed it. She set the threshold at a third of the spike's peak, so the steady drain all around them would not trip it and anything with that spike's shape would. The haptic alert had died in the crash along with half the screen, so the best she could give it was a single chirp, once, on a match. She kept her paw where she could cover the speaker.

They went on in single file, slow and silent. The trees grew older and more twisted the further they went, and the half-light thinned toward dusk though it was barely midmorning. Then the scraping stopped. They stood and waited for it to start again, and it did not, and the silence after it was worse than the sound had been.

Zax found its source a minute later. In a hollow between two great roots lay a Crustle, a big, crab-like Pokémon carrying a slab of rock on its back for a shell, tipped over on its side in the mud. The rock was split from top to bottom. A long furrow behind it showed where it had dragged itself, a little way at a time, toward the edge of the wood. Its breathing was quick and shallow. Its eyes rolled toward them and away, wide with a fear so deep it could not even try to run. Nothing had eaten it. Something had simply knocked it out of the way.

Kenji had hopped down to a low branch to see over the roots. Now he let out a choked squawk and flattened himself to the bark. Zax had already moved. "Down," he breathed, and swept the others back behind the nearest root with one arm, the appendages on his head standing straight out, and pressed them flat in the mud.

Something blotted out what little light came through the canopy. It slid between the trees above them, huge and fast and silent but for the slow, heavy beat of six wings: a three-headed dragon, dark purple and black, its long body coiling as it turned. A Hydreigon. Its outer heads swung left and right over the forest floor, and its eyes burned a dull, sick red. Elara felt it before she understood it: a fury with nothing behind it, as if everything else in the creature had burned away. She pressed both hands over her mouth until it had passed.

The tricorder chirped once before Anya smothered it against the fur of her chest. A match. On the screen, the trace she had built from the spike stood far above its threshold, dead on the band, and it was moving: it climbed as the dragon slid overhead, peaked right above them, and fell away behind it.

The Hydreigon had not seen them. It flew a wide, slow circle above the hollow, heads darting, as if it were hunting for something in particular. Then it swung away between the trunks, and they heard its wingbeats fade, and turn, and begin, unhurriedly, to come back. They had found the source of the fear. It was far beyond anything they could fight, and they were lying in the middle of its hunting ground.

Zax counted under his breath through the next circuit, and the one after it. "Ninety seconds," he murmured as the wingbeats faded a third time. "Give or take. Same circle every time. It is hunting for something, and it is not us. Yet."

In the hollow, the Crustle was still breathing, quick and shallow, right out in the open where the next pass would find it. Eva looked at it and could not look away. Starfleet did not leave the wounded behind. The rule had never come with a clause about species.

"We can't just leave it," Anya whispered. The flames on her neck had sunk low, and they were shaking.

"Sentiment is a tactical liability, Captain," Zax breathed. "We extract ourselves. We re-evaluate from safety. We are in no position to render aid."

Before Eva could answer, Elara's eyes went wide. Something had come through the static, sharp and bright: pain, and a fear that closed around her chest like a fist, and under both of them a plea with no words in it at all. It was the Crustle. Without thinking, she pushed back at it with every bit of calm she could find, the way she might have squeezed someone's hand. In the hollow, the Crustle's breathing slowed, just a little. Nobody noticed but her.

Eva made her decision. "Zax, Anya, get it upright on the next far pass. T'Vella, Elara, watch for my signal. We move the second it's clear."

Zax's jaw tightened, but he nodded.

They waited. The wingbeats came round, passed over them, and faded toward the far side of the hollow. "Now," Eva said, and Zax and Anya went over the root together. Lucario muscle and Typhlosion bulk set against the split shell, one heave, two, and the Crustle rocked back onto its legs with a grinding of stone. It stood there swaying, gave them one long, dazed look, and scuttled off into the undergrowth faster than anything that size had a right to move.

"It's coming round again!" Kenji hissed from his branch.

"Move," Eva said. "Quiet as you can."

They slipped out of the hollow the way they had come, low and fast, every step placed. It was a good retreat, the kind they had drilled a hundred times, and for twenty paces it worked. Then, behind them, they heard a short, sharp snort.

Eva looked back. Above the hollow, one of the Hydreigon's heads had stopped dead. It tilted, nostrils flaring, tasting the air. She knew what it had found before she let herself think it. Leaf's voice came back to her from the hillside, low and dry: *Like smoke in fur. You can't wash it off.* Whatever this wood smelled of, it did not smell of ozone and scorched metal and a dead starship.

All three heads snapped toward them. Six red eyes locked on, and the forest broke its silence with a triple roar.

Kenji was already in the air. He had gone up to keep eyes on it, the way he would have done for any of them, and that made him the highest, brightest thing in the wood. The Hydreigon's left head opened its jaws. A blast of black, crackling energy lanced up between the trunks and struck him square, and Kenji gave one thin, pained squawk before his wings folded and he dropped out of the air like a stone.

"Kenji!"

Eva was moving before she had decided to. Quick Attack took her across the forest floor in a white blur, and she caught him out of the air in her jaws a breath before he hit the ferns, as gently as she had ever held anything. He did not move. Anya was there a heartbeat later, arms out. "Give him here!" Eva let him go, and Anya tucked him against her chest, well clear of the flames.

"It's coming!" Zax yelled.

"Left!" Elara screamed.

They threw themselves left without a single question. A beam of black energy tore through the space where they had been standing and blew a crater in the forest floor, showering them with mud and splinters.

Elara had shouted before it fired. Before it had even turned its head. Eva rolled to her feet, and the pieces fell together all at once: the calm in the fields, the terror from deep in the wood, the pressure before every word they spoke. It was not a malfunction. It was a new sense coming online, and what it caught was intent, a heartbeat before the act.

"Elara!" Eva barked. "Keep calling it! Guide us!"

They ran, weaving through the trees with the Hydreigon crashing along the canopy above them, and Elara called it every step of the way, her voice cracking. "It's looking at Anya! Your flames, it's watching your flames!" Anya swore in Spanish and clamped down hard, and the bright collar of fire on her neck guttered to almost nothing. "Right side! It's going wide, it wants us out in the open—" Zax took the raw warning and turned it into a route, swerving them away from a gap in the trees and down into a narrow gully between two ancient trunks.

But the Hydreigon was faster than them, and stronger, and it was no longer trying to catch them. It was herding them. The gully narrowed, bent, and ended in a sheer face of wet rock too high to climb. They spun around. The dragon hung in the air at the mouth of the gully, three heads weaving apart and together, in no hurry at all.

"Dead end!" Zax planted his feet in front of them and tore the phaser out of the satchel at Anya's hip. "No flank, no exit. Protocol says maximum firepower. I'll cut us a way out."

"No," Eva snapped. "Wait for my command!"

Zax's paw tightened on the grip, but he held, and he did not waste the wait. He had seconds, if that. His thumb went to the controls without his eyes leaving the dragon. Wide dispersal would lay the beam across both wings, and the membranes looked thin; burn enough of them and the thing would come down. But the charge had read *low* since the crash, and spread that wide it would do little more than scorch something this size. He closed the aperture to its narrowest instead. All of it on one spot. Put that into bone, in the right place, and with luck it would shatter whatever it hit and sear the wound shut on the way through, and this would be over in a single shot. He left the intensity on heavy stun, one click short of the thermal settings, where protocol said it stayed until somebody gave the order.

"Aperture minimum," he bit out. "On your word, Captain."

The Hydreigon's heads, which had been moving each on its own, stopped and turned to face them together, perfectly in step. Their jaws opened as one. Three points of black, crackling energy began to grow, pulling the grey light in toward them until the rock face behind the team went darker than the rest of the wood. Their fur stood on end. Elara made a small, broken sound, and Eva did not need to ask her what it meant. The three spheres drew together into one, a churning ball of darkness that seemed to swallow the light around it.

Eva's eyes were on the left wing. The joint where it met the body flexed and locked with every beat, holding the whole massive creature steady in the air.

"Zax." She heard her own voice go flat and very calm, the way it always did when things were at their worst. "Left wing. The joint. Precision shot. One."

"Stun?" he shot back.

"Not on something that size. Narrow beam. Now!"

The phaser looked like a toy in his paw. Zax raised it, and Eva saw him glance at the charge out of pure habit, the small amber reading that had said *low* since the crash. Then the emitter glowed, and with a sharp, clean hiss that belonged to no forest anywhere, a thin orange beam lanced across the gully. It did not hit the broad membrane of the wing. It hit the joint, exactly where she had said.

The Hydreigon shrieked. The wound did not bleed. For a moment the point of impact glowed a sick, bright orange, and then the joint simply set, flash-fused into a blackened knot that stank of ozone and scorched flesh. The wing locked. The great body lurched, tipped, and went into a violent spin, and the ball of darkness between its heads tore loose.

A thick, ragged beam ripped out of the spin and scythed past them, close enough that they felt the heat on their faces, and carved a molten trench ten meters long through the side of the gully. Two ancient trees were there, and then they were not. The damp in the air flashed to steam with a deafening hiss, and the shockwave threw all six of them flat.

"On your feet!" Eva yelled, scrambling up. "Go! Go!"

They ran past the dragon as it thrashed in the undergrowth, too lost in pain and its own wild spinning to turn on them. Nobody looked back. Zax took point with the phaser still in his paw, Elara called the way between gasps, and behind them the Murky Wood was full of noise at last: branches crashing, roaring, the hiss of the steaming trench. Somewhere ahead, between the trunks, the grey began to thin toward daylight.

Anya ran with Kenji held tight against her chest. He weighed nothing at all, and she could not feel him breathing.
