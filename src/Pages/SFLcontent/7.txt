The bridge was alive.

Eva felt it the moment her forepaws touched it. It gave slightly under her weight, as no deck plate ever would, and the rail beside her was not a rail but a living vine as thick as her leg, its bark worn smooth by a great many hands and paws. There were leaves on it. In the gaps between the woven planks she could see the river sliding past below, black and silver under the two moons.

Anya, behind her, put one paw on the rail and left it there all the way across.

On the far bank the town closed around them. The paths ran between the great trees and the low, rounded houses, lit at every bend by the amber crystals on their posts, and the air smelled of damp earth, woodsmoke and something sweet that Eva could not name. It was quiet. Most of the windows were dark. Behind a few of them a lamp still burned, and once, from inside a house they passed, someone laughed softly and was shushed.

Not everyone was asleep.

A door stood open at the end of a lane of shuttered stalls, and in it an old Pokémon was taking in the last of a day's goods: a stooped, heavy-shouldered figure with a basket on each arm, and beside it a smaller one holding the lamp. The light caught the six of them as they came past. The small one stared. The old one only looked, the way people look at weather coming.

Then its eyes found Eva, and stopped.

It did not say anything to her. It turned its head away to the small one and spoke low, the way you speak about something you are not meant to be speaking about, and with the ears she had now Eva heard every word of it.

"An Absol," it said. "Tonight. Of all the nights."

"Gran—"

"They don't come to a town for nothing. They only come before something bad." The baskets went down. The old one put a hand on the small one's shoulder and drew it back inside. "You've had your look. In."

The door closed. The lamp behind it went out.

Eva kept walking. She did not let her stride change; she had walked past worse looks than that on worse worlds. But it went in, and it stayed. She did not know what an Absol was supposed to mean here. She knew what she had heard in that old voice. It was not hatred. It was dread.

*They only come before something bad.*

Something bad had already come. It had come yesterday, out of the sky, at sundown. It had been hers.

Ahead of her, Leaf glanced back once over his shoulder. He did not say anything either. Then he turned and went on, and so did she.

They walked on, deeper into the town, with Cinder's tail lighting the way at the front.

Elara walked close to Eva's side, looking at the windows: at the few lamps still burning behind them, at a toy left on a step, at washing hung between two branches to dry. "People live here," she said softly, when Eva looked at her. "Families. Look at it."

Kenji had gone back to Zax's shoulder and stayed there, his head turning to follow every lit window. Zax walked with his ears forward and his paw loose at his side, noting every doorway, every alley mouth, every place a team of six might be cut off from the way they had come. T'Vella walked as if she were counting the houses, and quite possibly was.

And Anya walked with her head back, staring up.

The trees grew larger the further in they went. The houses in their trunks went up two and three storeys, their windows set into the bark in spirals, joined by ramps of living branch that someone had persuaded to grow level. Anya kept stopping to look at a join, a buttress, a bend in a beam that carried weight it should not have been able to carry, and Eva kept having to wait for her. Once, under her breath, she said, "*Increíble.*" Once she said, "They're not even braced. Captain, look, they're not even *braced*," and Eva said, "I see it, Chief," and steered her on by the elbow.

Then the path opened, and they all stopped together.

The tree at the heart of Havenwood was not a tree in any sense Eva had words for. It went up out of the middle of the town like a column holding up the sky, so broad at the base that the path took a full minute to curve around it, and so tall that its upper branches were lost in the dark, somewhere above the moons' light. Its roots ran out across the ground in ridges taller than Rumble. Light crystals glowed in its bark at every height, dozens of them, hundreds, like the lights of a starbase seen from approach.

Carved into the trunk, at the top of a broad stair of root, was a great arched door. Above it, cut deep into the wood and picked out in some pale metal that caught the lamplight, was a symbol: a pair of spread wings around a single round stone.

Anya's eyes went straight from the door to the badge on Cinder's strap and back again. It was the same shape.

"The Guildhall," said Cinder, unnecessarily, and led them up the stair.

The doorway was not a door at all but a hollow: an arch opened straight through the living trunk, deep enough that for a few strides they walked in a tunnel of the tree's own wood. The walls were left as they had grown, grained and bark-dark and untouched. Only the floor had been worked, smoothed and levelled until it was as flat as a deck. A few steps in, the craft began: patterns cut into the floor and running up the walls on either side, long sweeping lines and leaf shapes and interlocking spirals, worn soft at the edges by generations of feet and paws.

Then the arch opened out, and they were standing in the heart of the tree.

It was an atrium, round and high, hollowed out of the living wood. In one corner stood a low stage, empty at this hour. Halls ran off to the left and right into the dark. In the middle of the floor stood two great boards side by side, so thick with papers and scrolls that they hung off the edges in layers and stirred in some faint draft from above. Behind them, drawn right into the curve of the ceiling, was a map: an enormous one, coastlines and rivers and forests and ridges spread across the wood overhead, with the great tree itself marked at its heart. High up the walls, where the trunk began to narrow, skylights had been opened through the tree's sides. They were made for daylight, Eva guessed: sun striking down through the great canopy overhead and glancing off branch and bark until some of it found its way in. Tonight, under all those leaves, the moons could not reach them. The skylights were only darker shapes high on the walls, and the room below was lit only by light crystals set into the walls, the same steady amber as the posts outside.

On the far side, a broad ramp curved up around the wall to a second level, rising in one long, gentle turn with not a single step in it. Opposite it, another just like it went down. Even in the half-dark Eva noticed how easy the slope was, and how wide. Built, she thought, for every kind of body a guild like this might have to carry up and down it: four legs or two, wings, wheels, someone hurt and leaning on a friend.

By day, Eva thought, this place would be full of noise. By night it creaked gently, like a ship, as the great tree moved around them in the wind.

There was a desk near the door. Behind it, on a perch, a round, black-headed bird with a fan-shaped tail sat with its head tucked under one wing, fast asleep.

Cinder walked straight past it.

The bird's head came up so fast it nearly fell off its perch. "Wh— Cinder! You're back! Is that— did you— are *those*—" It had seen them. Its feathers stood up all at once until it was half again its own size. "*Who* are—"

"Guildmaster's expecting us," said Cinder, without breaking stride. "Go back to sleep."

The bird opened its beak, shut it, and stared at them all the way to the ramp. Eva had the strong impression that it would very much have liked to write something down.

The ramp wound up through the inside of the tree for a long time. They passed doorways cut into the wood, closed, with names carved over them. They passed a landing where a window looked out over the whole of the sleeping town, lamps and river and dark roofs laid out like a chart. The air grew warmer as they climbed, and began to smell of smoke. Not woodsmoke. Something heavier and drier, like hot stone.

At the top was a single door. Cinder knocked once, waited, and opened it.

The room beyond was simple. Shelves lined the curving walls from floor to ceiling, crowded with scrolls, bound books, and stones: rough crystals and polished slices of rock, a fist of something that glittered like fused glass, a shelf of what looked like fossils. There was no desk. In the middle of the floor was a broad, shallow hearth of flat stones, and in it a bed of coals burned low and red. Above it, a round vent opened through the roof to let the smoke out, and down through it, faintly, came a thin grey thread of moonlight that had found its way through the leaves. The rest of the light came from the coals, and from a few crystals set into the walls between the shelves, turned low.

In the red glow of the hearth, beside the coals, sat a Torkoal.

He was large and very old. His shell was dark and rough as a burned log, cracked across and furred in places with moss, and from the vents in it a thin, steady smoke rose and drifted up toward the hole in the roof. His eyes were half shut. He did not look as if he had moved in hours. He did not look, either, as if he had slept.

"Guildmaster Ember-Smoke," Cinder said.

The voice that had told Eva, on the ridge, to make no mistake, was gone. In its place was something formal and careful and young. Cinder stepped forward into the light and bowed, and held out the scroll that Leaf had been writing on all night.

"Team Cinder, reporting as ordered. We went out to the crater this morning. We found the thing that fell." He hesitated. "We tracked a trail away from it, east, all day. They were at the end of it, on the ridge above the river. Six of them. They carry the same energy we felt at the site." A small pause. "Given what fell, and where, we thought it best to bring them straight to you."

The tortoise's eyes opened.

He took the scroll from Cinder slowly, as he seemed to do everything, and unrolled it on the hearthstones in the light of the coals. His eyes went down Leaf's neat lines faster than Eva would have believed: the crater, the metal, the wrong air, the six of them, whatever Leaf had written when his pencil stopped. A puff of smoke rose from his back. He rolled the scroll up again, set it aside, and then he looked at them for the first time, one after another, and his gaze came to rest on Eva.

"So," he said. His voice was slow and low, a sound from somewhere very deep. "You are the ones who drew the anger of the great Sky Guardian." A pause, long enough for the coals to settle. "The one they call Rayquaza."

The name landed like a blow.

*Rayquaza.* A name for the green serpent in the sky. A name for the light in its jaws and the lash it had dragged along her ship and the beam it had sent through the heart of her engines. A name for what had killed a hundred and thirty-five of her people in the time it took to scream.

Eva felt the cold rise in her before she could stop it, a fury so clean and so complete that for a moment she could not see the room. Beside her, Zax's ears went flat against his skull and both paws closed into fists. He had a name for the target now, and she could feel him filing it. Anya's fire came up all along her neck in a single hard flare, bright enough to throw their shadows across the shelves, and she pulled it down again with a sound in her throat like a growl. T'Vella did not move at all, except that something in the line of her jaw drew tight, and stayed tight. On Zax's shoulder, Kenji had made himself very small.

Elara looked at the floor.

Eva made herself breathe. She put the fury down, the way she had been trained to put things down on a bridge, where they would wait, and she stepped forward into the light of the hearth and inclined her head to the old tortoise by the fire.

"Guildmaster," she said. "My name is Eva. These are my crew." Her voice was steady. She was glad of it. "We never meant to bring trouble to your town. Our arrival here was… not our choice."

The Guildmaster looked at her for a long moment. Smoke rose from his shell.

"Not your choice," he repeated, as if tasting it. Then, to Cinder, without looking away from Eva: "Thank you, Cinder. You have done well, all three of you. Your report is noted. Go and get some sleep."

Cinder bowed again. "Yes, Guildmaster." At the door he paused, and looked back once at the six of them by the fire, and Eva could not read what was in his face. Then he went out, and Leaf after him, and Rumble last, ducking low under the frame, and the door closed softly behind them.

They were alone with the old tortoise and the fire.

"Cinder tells me," the Guildmaster said, "that you have lost your memories. That you cannot say where you are from." His voice was carefully neutral. "It happens, to those who wander lost out of a Mystery Dungeon. It is common enough. But it is… unusual… for six to lose them all together. And it is more unusual still for them to come walking out of the woods on the day after a light like that one."

Eva looked at him. At the old, patient eyes. At the smoke going up toward the vent, and the coals, and the shelves of stones and scrolls around him. A room where someone sat up all night waiting to be told the truth.

She had lied to Cinder on the ridge, because she had had to. She found, standing here, that she did not want to lie to this one.

"Our memories are not gone, Guildmaster," she said quietly. Behind her, she felt rather than heard the others go still. "They are only… hard to fit to what we are now. Our home is very far from here. So far that the only way to travel from it was in a vessel that crossed the sky. It was never meant for a world like this one. It was damaged as it came in, and it fell." Her voice did not break. She did not let it. "There were many more of us aboard. We are all that are left. The rest of our crew… did not make it."

She did not tell him about Starfleet, or the Federation, or what they had been before the light. But what she told him was true, every word, and she let him hear that it was.

Behind her, very slightly, T'Vella shook her head. Once.

Eva did not need to turn to see it. She had asked for exactly that on a hillside a few hours ago: *if I cross a line, you say so.* Her first officer was keeping her word. *Nothing they did not already have,* Eva had told them, and she had just given this old Torkoal a little more than that.

She found she could not be sorry for it. He had watched their ship fall out of his sky. Whatever she had told him, his world had seen most of it already, and she suspected it would see a great deal more before they were done.

When she finished, the old tortoise was silent for a long time.

He looked up, past them, at the vent in the roof and the thin grey thread of moonlight in it, and his eyes grew distant, as if he were reading something written a long way off. Far back in his memory the old stories were stirring, the ones the Guild kept and the young ones only half believed: tales of beings summoned from beyond the sky in times of great crisis. One, lost and confused, wandering out of a Mystery Dungeon with no memory of the road behind them; that much was a story he had heard more than once in a long life. But six, together, arriving on the back of a silver streak that had made the Sky Guardian itself come down in anger…

That was not a story he had heard before.

"A vessel that crosses the sky," he said at last, slowly. "From a very distant land indeed."

He looked at them again: at the Absol with her head held up and her grief held down, at the Lucario standing a half step in front of her without seeming to know he was doing it, at the Typhlosion with her fire banked low, the tall Gardevoir, the small Kirlia, the Starly on the Lucario's shoulder. At the way they stood. Six of them, close together, each facing a slightly different way.

"You are lost," the Guildmaster said. "You have no home. But you are not helpless. I can see that much from here." Smoke rose from his back, slow and steady. "You stand like a team that has stood together a long time. You carry a great loss, and you have not let it break you. That is rare. Havenwood can give you a place to rest and mend. And, if you want it, a chance to show what you are worth."

Eva did not answer at once. It was a great deal to be offered by someone who had known them for under a quarter of an hour.

"Forgive me, Guildmaster," she said. "But you don't know us. We came out of the same sky as whatever made your Sky Guardian angry. On the way here, one of your people took one look at me and sent her grandchild indoors." She held his eyes. "Why would you open your doors to us?"

Smoke went up toward the vent. For a while the only sound was the coals.

"Because this has happened before," Ember-Smoke said.

Behind her, Eva felt Zax go still.

"Not like this," the old Torkoal went on. "Never quite like this. But there are stories in this Guild older than this tree, and they all begin the same way. The world is in trouble. And out of nowhere, out of a storm or out of the sky, someone arrives who was never one of us before, and wakes up as one of us." His eyes moved over them, slowly. "Lost. Confused. Carrying a life from somewhere else. And when the trouble comes, they are the ones standing in front of it."

"And what became of them?" T'Vella asked. "The strangers."

"The stories do not agree." He looked down at the coals. "Some went home when it was over, they say. Some chose to stay." A long breath of smoke. "And some of the stories simply stop."

Nobody said anything to that.

"As for the Absol," he said, and for the first time something like dry humor moved in the old voice, "there are those who say your kind only come before a calamity. Perhaps. But the stories I am speaking of are older than that one, and in them the strangers did not bring the calamity." He paused. "They came because of it."

"And if the stories are wrong?" said Zax.

"Then I am an old fool who gave six tired strangers a bed for the night." The smallest movement of the heavy head. "I have been called worse." His gaze came back to Eva. "This world has troubles of its own, lately. Cinder will have told you that much. If the old stories are even half true, I would rather have you inside these walls than wandering outside them. It is a gamble." The coals settled. "At my age, you learn which gambles are worth taking."

"Charity is a short road," he went on, more briskly. "If you mean to make a life here, to earn your keep and find your purpose, there is a longer one. You can register with the Guild as a new exploration team. Put your strength to work for this town, and for the places around it." The coals ticked softly in the hearth. "But the choice is yours."

Eva looked back at her crew.

She did not need to ask. Zax gave one short, sharp nod: a Guild meant structure, allies, a base to work from instead of a ridge in the dark. Anya's fire, low all evening, came up a little brighter along her neck; a Guild meant tools, materials, someone else's drawings to look at. T'Vella inclined her head the smallest degree, which from her was as good as a signature. Elara gave a small, tired, hopeful smile. And Kenji, on Zax's shoulder, nodded so hard he nearly fell off.

Eva turned back to the fire.

For a moment, the words would not come.

She had accepted assignments all her life. Every one of them had come down a chain of command with the Federation at the top of it: a flag she understood, an oath she had sworn, people she trusted to have thought it through. This had none of that. An old tortoise by a fire, inside a tree, on a world with no name, offering six people who had been Starfleet officers a day ago a place in something she knew almost nothing about. Once she said yes, there would be no going back to the ridge. They would belong to someone again, and she could not see where that road went.

She looked at the coals. Then, once more, at her crew, and found every one of them already looking back at her. Waiting for her call, the way they always had.

That, at least, had not changed.

"We accept, Guildmaster," she said. "We'll be your newest exploration team."
