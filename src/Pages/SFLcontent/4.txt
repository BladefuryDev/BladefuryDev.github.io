The shelter emitter died at dawn.

The dome thinned from the top down like breath off a window, there was a soft hiss, and the morning came in all at once: cold air, wet leaves, and a hundred birds.

Anya was at the emitter before the hiss had ended. She turned it over and read the indicator, which read nothing. "Cell's flat," she said. "That's it for her." She wiped the dew off the casing, folded the arms down, and packed it into the satchel as carefully as if it still worked.

"It is dead weight, Chief," Zax said.

"It's Starfleet hardware, and this is somebody's forest." She pulled the strap tight. "We don't leave it lying on other people's planets. Contamination protocol."

Nobody argued. It was the first thing any of them had said that morning that sounded like the day before yesterday.

Eva had not slept. She got up expecting to pay for it, and did not. Her paws, raw by midnight, were only paws. The ache the shockwave had left in her was gone. She stretched, and nothing hurt.

She looked at Zax. He had not slept either. She had listened to him not doing it.

"How do you feel?"

"Rested," he said, as if reporting a fault.

"It is the fruit," T'Vella said. She opened her eyes as she spoke, which settled nothing about whether she had slept. "I have no instrument to confirm it. But six subjects are in better condition than a night on cold ground can account for, and there is one new variable."

Anya already had the tricorder on one of the berries, frowning at the cracked screen. "She's right, as far as this thing can tell. Sugars, water, bio-active compounds, and an energy signature it can't classify at all. It's calling it Bio-Restorative Fruit Zero-One and giving up." She swept the scanner over herself, then over Kenji, who was asleep on his back with his feet in the air. "Our readings changed overnight too. And one for your list, Commander. None of us has had a drink since the bridge. Is anybody thirsty?"

Nobody was. They looked at one another and found that out.

"Hydration's normal across the board," Anya said. "There isn't that much water in a berry. It's putting back more than it carries."

"Food, water and medicine on one bush," T'Vella said. "I find that too convenient to be comfortable."

"I'll take convenient," Anya said. "I've had the other kind."

"Then that's our first order of business," Eva said. "We take all of it."

They stripped the bush. Elara climbed it to reach the middle and handed the clusters down. Kenji woke up, ate four, and fell over. Anya had to shift the ration tubes to fit the berries in the satchel. None of them could eat the tubes anymore. She kept them anyway.

"Protocol one," Eva said, when the satchel was shut. "A position we can hold. A satchel of berries is a few days, not a plan, so I still want to know where the water is. Whatever lives here will know too. Zax, you have point. Kenji, short hops, stay in sight. Let's move out."

They left the dead dome's ring of flattened grass behind, and within a hundred meters the forest by daylight was not the forest they had crawled through in the dark.

It was enormous. The trees that had been walls in the night were columns now, wider than a turbolift, rising to a canopy so high that the birds in it were only sound. Ferns stood taller than Elara. On one fallen trunk grew a shelf of orange fungus that Anya scanned, said "No," and walked around.

Walking was easier than yesterday. When Eva watched her paws, she fell. When she looked where she was going, the body took the ground on its own, in a long low stride that ate distance. She fell once before mid-morning. Yesterday it had been four times in ten minutes.

Kenji was learning to land. About half the time now he arrived on the branch he had aimed for, and he announced every success. When he missed for the third time that hour, Zax put out a paw without looking up, caught him, and set him on his shoulder without breaking stride.

"I had that one," Kenji said.

"You did not."

"I had most of it."

They walked east, away from the crater.

It was Zax who found the water. He stopped with one ear turned. "Running water," he said. "Two hundred meters. Maybe less. Downslope." He frowned. "I should not be able to hear that."

"Fault report noted," Eva said. "Take us there."

It was a stream three meters across, shallow and quick over round stones, with a bank of flat rock on the near side and a steep hill behind it. Anya scanned it before anyone could kneel. "Clean. Same odd trace as the berries, fainter."

"Then we learn to drink while it's a choice," Eva said. "The berries won't last forever. Two at a time. The rest watch the trees."

Nobody had told them how. Zax cupped water in his paws and most of it ran out between fingers that were not quite fingers. Anya put her face in the stream. Kenji dipped his beak, tipped his head back, and looked astonished that this worked. T'Vella made a cup of her three-fingered hands that did not leak, which surprised no one.

Eva stood at the edge and understood that she had no hands at all.

She lowered her head, and the body knew what to do. The water was cold and tasted of stone. She was not thirsty; she drank a little anyway, to know that she could. When she stopped, she saw what was looking up at her.

The stream ran smooth over the rock, and it held a face. White fur. A dark, narrow muzzle. A blade of horn curving up from the side of the head. Red eyes.

She had seen her own paws. It was not the same. She looked for herself in the water and found a stranger's face wearing her expression. The tiredness was hers. She watched the stranger's ears go back, felt her own go, and that was the worst part.

She counted to three in Russian. Then she lifted her head.

The others had found it too. It was very quiet on the bank.

Anya was staring down at a wide, short-muzzled face with a collar of fire behind it. "Mi abuela," she said at last, hoarsely, "would have had a great deal to say about this."

T'Vella studied her reflection like a specimen, turning her head one way, then the other. "Structurally improbable," she said. "I do not see how the neck supports it." A pause. "I had expected to object more strongly."

Elara touched the two pink points that rose through her helmet of green hair, as though they might belong to someone else. She said nothing. After a moment she put her hand flat on the water and broke the picture up.

Zax glanced down once, as flatly as at a readout. Then he turned his back on the stream and did not look again.

"Oh, no," Kenji said.

He was standing in two centimeters of water with his wings half out, peering down between his own feet.

"Lieutenant?"

"Captain, I'm adorable." He sounded appalled. "Look at this. Look at the little face. I have a little white face. Nobody is ever going to take an order from this." He tilted his head, and the small grey bird in the stream tilted its head, and he made a noise of pure despair. "I'm *round*."

Anya laughed, a short cracked bark that kept coming, and she sat down on the bank with her paws over her eyes and her shoulders shaking. It was not entirely laughter. Nobody mentioned that. Elara was smiling behind her hand, and even T'Vella's head had inclined a degree.

Eva let them have it. Then she said, "You'll take the orders, Lieutenant. I'll give them. That's the arrangement."

"Aye, Captain," said the small round bird, with dignity.

"Contact," said Zax.

He had not raised his voice. Every head on the bank came around.

"The tall grass, across the water. Four of them. Small, fast, coming straight in." His ears were flat forward. "They're not curious."

They came out of the grass in a line: four low purple animals the size of large cats, with long bare tails and front teeth that showed even with their mouths shut. They did not slow at the water.

The smallest came last, half a step behind the others, and Eva knew it. She had watched its eyes all night, the last to leave. It had gone to tell someone. Here they were.

"Circle!" she snapped. "Backs together! Zax, weapons fr—"

Zax's paw was already at the satchel on Anya's shoulder, on the flap, on the grip of the phaser inside it.

"No. Belay that! Hold your fire!" One cell. A few shots, if he was careful, and after that nothing, ever. "We are not spending it on these! Hand to hand!"

They formed up. It was a good formation, drilled a hundred times on holodecks, designed by bipeds for bipeds, and it lasted about two seconds.

Zax dropped into an Andorian guard, built to meet an opponent's center of mass. This one's center of mass was at his shin, and it went under his hands. Eva called the flank and found she could not point. She swiped at the nearest one, a human punch thrown with the wrong limb, and it ducked and went for her hind leg.

Kenji went up off the stones and came down on the back of another, pecking and shrieking, and for one glorious moment had it running in circles. Then its tail whipped round and took him off, and he hit the stones and rolled. Elara and T'Vella had nothing to hit with and could only give ground, T'Vella between Elara and the nearest teeth, backing at exactly the speed it advanced, perfectly calm.

Zax caught his on the second pass: a short, perfect strike that would have put a Klingon on the deck. The animal tumbled across the rock, got up, and came back. He hit it again. It came back again, got past his hands and onto his forearm, teeth in, and Zax, who held three Academy combat records, roared and spun like a man fighting a coat.

"Zax!" Anya went for him with a bellow, every kilo of her.

The one Kenji had ridden turned from him and jumped straight at her face.

There was no time for a decision, only the heat. It had sat under her breastbone since the crater, held down like a hatch against a flooding deck. She opened her mouth to roar. The hatch went.

What came out was a spray of white-orange embers, the way a grinder throws them. It caught the animal in midair, and what missed spattered the rock and smoked. The thing shrieked, hit the ground smoking, and ran.

The other three stopped dead.

Zax did not stop. All the fury of the last minute came down his free arm in a single line, and he drove his open palm at the animal on the other. Something jumped the gap.

It was blue, bright even in daylight, and it cracked like a relay blowing. The animal flew clear across the bank and landed with its fur standing straight out, less a rat than a purple dandelion with teeth. It scrabbled up, gave a thin, deeply wounded squeak, and fled into the grass after the first.

The last two looked at Anya, smoke curling from her mouth. They looked at Zax's paw, which was glowing. They looked at each other.

The bigger one flattened itself to the rock, ears down, and backed away on its belly, squeaking in short, earnest bursts, like someone explaining there had been a misunderstanding. Its tail met the water. It jumped straight up, came down facing the other way, and crossed the stream in a somersault.

That left the small one, the watcher, alone on the bank with six monsters it had personally recommended.

It looked at Eva. Eva looked back. It gave her one squeak, tiny and high, turned to follow its friends, ran straight into a stone, sat down, got up, and went around the stone with enormous dignity. Then it bolted, and the grass closed behind it.

Nobody moved. The air smelled of ozone and burnt hair.

"What," Kenji said, from the stones, in a very small voice, "was *that*?"

Nobody had an answer. Out in the grass the squeaking went on for a while, receding, in the tone of an argument about whose idea this had been.

Eva breathed out. Her legs wanted to shake, and she did not let them. The question would keep. Her people might not.

"Report," she said. "Injuries first."

It came back in order. Kenji: winded, bruised, and offended. Elara and T'Vella: untouched. Anya: nothing. Eva had a scuff on her hind leg she had not felt, the fur roughed up and nothing underneath. Zax had taken the worst of it. He held out his forearm already braced for what he would find, and parted the fur over two crescents of tooth marks. There was no blood. The skin was dented and darkening into a bruise, and that was all.

He held the arm out for T'Vella without being asked, and she bent over it, and Eva saw the moment her hand went to her side for a medkit that was a lump of fused plastic in a locker in a crater.

"There is nothing to treat," T'Vella said. She pressed beside the marks, and he barely flinched. "An animal that size, hanging from a human forearm by its teeth, would have opened it to the muscle. These bodies are considerably harder to damage than the last ones." She let go. "Noted."

"Later," Zax said. He was turning his other paw over and back. A faint blue shimmer still clung to it, fading. "I want to know what I just did."

"So do I." Anya had her own paw flat on her chest. "That heat. It's still there, but it's less. It went *down* when I..." She gestured at the scorched rock. "When I did that."

Zax nodded slowly. "The anger. It is quieter now. It dropped the moment the discharge left my hand."

T'Vella straightened. "Describe the sensation before the event. Precisely."

"Pressure," Anya said.

"Yes," said Zax. "Pressure."

"And after."

"Less pressure."

"Then neither of you is angry." T'Vella folded her hands. "A state that builds without cause, that you can feel the level of, and that falls when you spend energy, is not an emotion. It is a reservoir." She looked from one of them to the other. "A battery, Chief. You have discharged part of it."

Anya stared at her. Then she pulled a berry out of the satchel, put it in her mouth, and stood with her eyes shut and her paw on her chest, as still as she had ever stood at a warp core diagnostic.

"It's coming back," she said. Her eyes opened. "Slowly. Like a trickle charge. I can feel the gauge." She turned the tricorder on the berries, then on the scorch mark. "And the signature this thing couldn't classify in the fruit is the same one coming off that rock. We eat it, we store it, and apparently we can throw it."

"I said last night that the parts that should be a weapon are," Zax said. He closed the paw. "I did not know how right I was."

Eva felt the whole situation turn over. Yesterday she had six unarmed castaways and one failing phaser. She had ordered a diagnostic on these bodies, and the forest had just run it for her.

"T'Vella. New priority one. I want everything about this: what it is, what feeds it, what triggers it, what it costs. We do not move from this bank until we have a baseline."

"Understood."

"Captain." It was Elara, twisting her fingers together. "You called them ferals. Non-sapient."

"I did."

"One of them was here last night. The small one. I saw you see it." She swallowed. "It watched us, and it left, and it came back with three more to the one place we'd have our heads down. That's a plan."

"It is also what wolves do," Zax said. "And a wolf is not a person."

"We do not know what they are," T'Vella said. "I would point out that we have been on this world for one night, and we have now set fire to one of its inhabitants and electrocuted another. I do not say we were wrong. I say the sample is small."

Eva had been over it while it was happening. "They didn't signal. They didn't posture. They didn't make a sound that was meant for us. They came for the weakest of us from cover and they left when it cost too much. I'll stand on feral." She looked at Elara. "Provisionally. Until one of them says otherwise. If anything in this forest so much as tries to talk to us, Counselor's rules apply and we all stand down. Clear?"

"Clear," Elara said, and some of the tightness went out of her hands.

"Good. Then let's find out what we are." Eva looked once at the grass across the stream, then turned her back on it. The bank would do: water in front, the hill behind, open rock all round. "Protocol two. We calibrate. The equipment has a weapons system, and none of us knows where the safety is. Until we do, we're a danger to each other. Anya, Zax, you're up. Do it again. On command."

It was not that simple.

Anya squared up to a boulder and reached for the anger. She thought about the animal coming at her face. She thought about the ship. She roared. A puff of black smoke came out, and a few cinders that died before they reached the ground. She coughed. "It's not working. I can't just *get* that angry. Not on purpose."

Zax put his palm out toward a tree and went back into the fight in his head. A flicker of blue ran across his pads and vanished. He tried again, teeth bared. Nothing. "I cannot produce it at will," he said. It cost him something to say it.

"Then you are approaching it incorrectly," T'Vella said.

They both looked at her.

"You are attempting to reproduce an emotion. The emotion was not the mechanism. It was only the moment you stopped holding the mechanism shut. Do not try to become angry. Find the reservoir, which you tell me you can feel, and *intend* for it to move."

Anya grunted, closed her eyes again, and left the animal out of it. The heat was where it always was, banked and steady, not angry at anything. She did not roar. She opened her mouth and thought of a valve, and of turning it a quarter.

A thin, even stream of embers ran out across the bank and spattered up the side of the boulder. The moss on it blackened and smoked. She shut the valve, and the stream stopped.

She opened her eyes, and a slow grin spread over her face, the first since the bridge. "Oh," she said. "Oh, that's a *valve*. I can work with a valve."

Zax turned back to his tree and stopped trying to hit it. He went looking for whatever had jumped the gap and found it in his chest and arms, a low hum he had taken for adrenaline. He asked it, as he would ask a muscle, to come to his hand.

Blue light gathered over the paw, brightest at the palm. It stuttered. He steadied, and it steadied. He held it for three slow breaths, then let it fade.

"Intention," he said. He flexed the paw. "It answers."

Eva breathed out. "Then that's the trigger for all of us. Zax, Anya, keep at it. I want control before I want power. The rest of you, find some room and find out what you have. From this morning, these are the ship's weapons."

They spread out along the bank.

For Kenji it came almost at once. A pilot spends his life feeling power move through a hull. He found it in his wings and put a little more into one downstroke than it needed. A fist of wind flattened a fern twenty meters away, and he was so startled he fell off his rock.

"Did you see that?" he yelled, from the ground. "Did anybody see that? I did a *wind*!"

"We saw," Zax said, without turning around.

Eva's came sideways. She was standing with her eyes half shut, finding nothing she could name, when something small and white ran up the trunk beside her head: a squirrel, more or less, with a blue stripe down its back and a tail twice its size. It stopped at the level of her nose, chewing.

Every nerve she had jumped. Then she was not standing by the tree.

She was ten meters down the bank in a crouch, claws out, heart going like a drum, with no memory of the ground in between. A white streak hung in the air where she had been, thinning.

The squirrel finished its berry, flicked its tail, and went on up the tree.

"Huh," Eva said. It had cost her almost nothing.

There was a name in her head that she had not put there, the way the berries had simply been food to T'Vella: *Quick Attack*. She filed it under things to ask when there was someone to ask.

A low chuckle came from behind her, the first she had heard from Kenji since the crater. He landed on a root beside her, too interested to announce it. "That was fast, Captain. That was faster than an impulse burst. What did you *do*?"

"I was feeling for it when that thing startled me," Eva said, aware that her helmsman had just watched her bolt from a squirrel. "I wanted to be away from it, quickly. That is exactly what happened."

"So you're saying it worked."

"I'm saying it's a viable maneuver, Lieutenant, and you will not be describing it to anyone."

"No, ma'am." His feathers gave a nervous flutter and smoothed down again. He pressed his beak shut, but a small wheezing snort escaped it anyway, and he turned it into a cough that fooled nobody.

T'Vella turned to a thick old tree at the edge of the bank and raised both hands toward it. She did not strain. She looked as if she were about to ask it a question.

The air between them bent.

A wave of violet light went out of her, wide as a corridor, and hit the trunk with a sound like a deck plate dropped from a height. The tree held for half a second. Then it split, low down, with a crack that sent every bird in the canopy into the air, and it came over in a long slow fall and struck the bank hard enough that Eva felt it through all four feet.

Splinters pattered down. A few last leaves came after them.

"Whoa," said Kenji, who had ended up behind Zax's leg. "Remind me to stay on her good side."

"Area effect," Zax said. "Uncontrolled. We will want to be behind you, Commander."

Anya had the tricorder up. "No mass, no projectile. It reads like a gravimetric shear the size of a shuttle bay." She lowered it. "Back home I'd have said somebody was being *psychic* at that tree."

"Vulcans are not psychic, Chief," T'Vella said. "We are touch telepaths. It requires contact, and consent."

Anya looked at the stump. "Tell that to the tree."

"Considerable force," T'Vella went on, as if she had not heard. "Poor range, and not selective. I intended to push the tree. I did not intend to push it that much." A beat. "The lightness I reported last night is gone. I do not miss it."

That left Elara.

She stood at the shallow end of the bank with her eyes shut and her small hands in fists. Nothing had happened. It kept not happening. She was trying to do to herself what she did to a duty roster: arrange it, and ask it nicely. Whatever was in her did not answer to asking nicely. Her shoulders had been climbing toward her ears for ten minutes.

Kenji noticed too. He flew the length of the bank and landed on her shoulder, perfectly. "Hey," he chirped, right beside her ear. "Just relax. You'll get it! It's easy once you—"

"Kenji, I am *trying* to *concentrate!*"

It came off her forehead: a soft pulse of pink light, no bigger than a hand, and it went through him like a ripple through a pond.

Kenji said, "Oh," and slid sideways off her shoulder.

He caught himself in the air, more or less, flew a wobbling loop with his head tipped over, and landed on a stone with his feet too far apart. "Whoa," he said. "Everything's sp— everything's spinning. Why are there two captains? One was plenty."

"Kenji!" Elara had both hands over her mouth. "Oh, no. Oh, I'm so sorry."

"I'm okay." He shook his head hard, blinked, and looked very carefully at Eva. "One captain. We're good."

Eva walked over. She did not hurry. Elara watched her come like a cadet watching a review board.

"Captain, it was an accident. A negligent discharge. I was frustrated, and it just came out. I didn't intend..." She stopped. "No. I did. For half a second I wanted him to *stop*, and that's what went. There was a pressure, here." She touched her forehead, under the green fringe of her hair. "I know what it feels like now. I think I can find it again. On purpose."

In Starfleet a negligent discharge was a formal reprimand and a week of remedial range time. Eva could hear exactly how that would sound out here, to a woman less than a meter tall who had run the duty roster for a hundred and forty-one people and was standing on the last of it.

"There's nothing to apologize for," she said, more softly than she had spoken since the bridge. "None of us was issued a manual. You found your trigger, and a hit that scrambles the target's balance. I'd have paid a great deal for that an hour ago." The others had stopped to listen. "We're all we have left. We learn together. We make our mistakes together, here, where it's only us. And we move forward together. Understood?"

"Understood," Elara said.

"Aye, Captain," said the others, not quite in unison.

"And Kenji."

"Captain?"

"Next time someone is concentrating, land somewhere else."

"Yes, ma'am."

They worked through what was left of the morning. Anya learned to open the valve by quarters. Zax learned to hold his light, and then to strike with it, and put a palm-sized scorch in a rock from arm's length. Kenji knocked ferns over until Eva made him stop. Elara found the pressure behind her forehead three times running and pushed it, gently, at a stone, which was not affected in the least, and she looked happier about that than she had about anything. T'Vella paced off the range of her wave, twice, and wrote the numbers on nothing, and kept them.

Before they left, T'Vella called Zax over and took his arm in both hands.

The bruise was gone. When she pressed where it had been, he did not flinch at all.

"Three hours," T'Vella said. Anya ran the scanner over the arm and held up the screen. "Accelerated cellular regeneration," T'Vella read, "at a rate consistent with nothing. He ate berries after the fight." She let go of the arm. "I will add it to the list."

"It's a long list," Anya said.

"It is. I find that preferable to a short one."

They had nothing to carry water in, and for once it did not matter: the satchel was full of berries, and the berries had kept them watered since the bridge. Eva put them back in order, and they crossed the stream on the stones and went on east, leaving the scorched boulder and the fallen tree for whatever lived there to make sense of. The sun climbed past noon and started down, and they kept walking.

From the back of the line, Eva could see the change. Yesterday they had flinched at every sound. Now Zax walked point with one paw loose at his side. Anya's flames stood bright and steady. Even Elara had her head up, watching the undergrowth like someone with something to throw at it.

They were not safe. They had driven off four animals the size of cats and felled a tree. But they were not helpless either, and that was the difference between a crew and a group of people waiting to die.

The grief had not gone anywhere. It came up in the silences: when Kenji went quiet on Zax's shoulder for half an hour, when Anya stood a moment too long with her paw on the dead emitter in the satchel. Eva let it come. It weighed the same whether she looked at it or not.

Every few hundred meters she gave the nod, and Kenji spiraled up through the branches to the sky above the canopy. Each time he came back with the same report.

"Trees, Captain. Trees and hills. Trees as far as I can see."

"Keep looking."

The light went gold, then amber. The birds went quiet by ones and twos, and other things began: a tick, a chirr, something far off that whooped twice and stopped. Through a gap in the canopy, the first of the two moons was already up, thin and white.

Eva called the halt at the top of a rise, where the trees stood a little apart. "Kenji. One more, before we lose the light. As high as you can get. I want lights, smoke, a break in the trees. Anything that isn't forest."

"Aye, Captain." He went up, a small dark shape against a sky the color of a bruise, and was gone.

"Chief," Zax said, while they waited. "Bank your fire."

Anya looked at him.

"I want to measure something. And I would as soon we were not visible from the next hill while I do it."

Last night it had taken everything she had to keep the fire down. She had slept curled on her side with her head bowed away from the others, so the flames on her neck pointed into the dark and whatever got away from her would scorch nothing but moss. Now she thought of the valve and turned it, and the flames sank to a dull red line and stayed there. She did not have to hold them. She turned it back a fraction, just to see, and they rose a finger's width and settled where she put them.

"Easy," she murmured, and banked them again.

The dark came in close around them. Zax held out his paw and filled it with light.

A steady blue glow, about what a hand lamp gave on its lowest setting. It lit their faces from below. Anya watched the tricorder's chronometer.

"Mark," she said. "One minute."

He let it go. "Continuous drain. One minute of that costs me about what a strike costs. It will do for short work. It would be a foolish way to cross a forest."

"And it's blue," Anya said. "Which is one more thing I'd like explained. Mine's fire. Fire I understand. What *is* that?"

"I have no idea, Chief."

"Add it to the list," said T'Vella, from the dark.

Then Kenji came down.

He came down far too fast, straight through the gap in the canopy, and landed in the leaf litter in a tumble of wings, talking before he was upright.

"Lights! Captain, lights! East, maybe eight, ten kilometers, past a ridge. Not stars. Not wildfire. They're orange, and they're steady, and they're in *lines*." He was on his feet now, hopping, every feather on end. "Straight lines, with gaps, like streets. Dozens of them. Captain, that's a town. It has to be a town."

Nobody spoke.

A town. Anya's flames leapt up before she could stop them. Elara's hands went to her mouth. Streets meant builders. Lights in lines meant someone who wanted to see where they were going at night. After two days of ash and silence and things with teeth, there were minds out there with their lamps lit.

And on the heels of it, cold and exact, came the rest.

General Order One.

Eva had sworn to it at twenty-two, in a hall in San Francisco, with her right hand raised. She had taught it. She had once relieved a good officer of duty for bending it. No exceptions, the instructors had liked to say, for anything you will ever want as badly as you want this.

"A town," she said quietly.

"Captain," said T'Vella.

"I know, Commander. Say it anyway."

T'Vella stepped into the red glow of Anya's fire. "The Directive is unambiguous. A civilization lit by flame is by every indication pre-warp. We are to observe only. Our distress does not alter that. It is precisely when a crew is in distress that the Directive is tested."

"I agree," Zax said. "Not for her reasons. We don't know their numbers, their weapons, or what they do with strangers. This morning four of the locals tried to eat us. I would want a day on that ridge before I let any of us walk down a lit street."

"With all due respect, Commander." Anya had come forward too, the satchel hanging heavy from her shoulder. "The Prime Directive was written for a starship. A working one, in orbit, with a hundred and forty-one people and a transporter room and the option to leave. We are six castaways with a cracked tricorder and one phaser cell. Our ship is a radioactive hole in the ground a day's walk west of here, and they *watched it fall*. The contamination's done. It was done before we woke up. We're not observers. We're survivors, and survivors need tools and shelter and somebody to tell them what's safe to eat. We don't get any of that hiding in the woods."

"This morning," T'Vella said, "you would not leave a dead power cell in the moss."

Anya opened her mouth, and shut it.

"I do not say it to score a point, Chief. I say it because you were right this morning. I should like to understand what has changed, other than that we now want something."

"What's changed is I can pack an emitter," Anya said, low. "I can't pack *us*."

"She's right." It was Elara, and her voice was steadier than it had been all day. "Both of them are. But, Commander, we've lost everything. Everyone. We've been walking for two days through a world with nobody in it but us. And there are people over that ridge. Isn't that the other half of the oath? We don't only swear not to interfere. We swear to seek out new life. I never read that as *seek it out, and then stay in the bushes*."

Eva listened to all four of them. They were all correct. That was what made it a command decision and not an argument.

Her duty to the Federation was not a thing she could take off. It went down through her like the keel through a ship. But the Federation was a long way off, in a sky Kenji could not read. Her duty to the five people standing in this clearing was here, and it had names, and one of them had had a rat's teeth in his arm that morning.

"The Directive assumes a position of power," she said at last. "It protects the people on the ground from the people in the ship. Look at us. We have no ship. We cannot leave. Here, *we* are the vulnerable ones. I don't believe the people who wrote that order ever pictured it being read from down here."

"An exception, Captain?" There was an edge in T'Vella's level voice. "Or a rationalization? The harm we may do does not shrink because we are desperate. We are carrying theta residue on our fur into a town lit by lamps."

Eva held her first officer's eyes and found nothing there she could honestly argue with.

"You're right," she said. "It may be a rationalization. I have been a captain long enough to know I can't always tell from the inside. So you will tell me. That's an order, Commander. If I cross a line down there, you say so, in front of anyone."

T'Vella inclined her head. "I will."

"So hear me, all of you." She drew herself up, four paws square on a strange hill. "We do not reveal where we come from. Nothing comes out of that satchel. We approach quietly, we watch from the edge for as long as the edge will hide us, and we make contact only if we must. And if they are this morning's animals with lamps, we withdraw, and Zax gets his day on the ridge."

"I have five people left out of a hundred and forty-one, and I will not bury one of them under a tree out of respect for a regulation that cannot see us from where it is. That is my decision, and it is mine alone. If Starfleet ever asks, you followed orders. We proceed."

For a moment there was only the ticking of the night insects.

Then the breath went out of Anya all at once, and her fire came up gold. Elara's eyes were bright. Kenji turned a complete circle on the spot and had to be still on purpose.

T'Vella gave one short nod, the nod of an officer who had argued her case and lost it fairly. She would follow the order, and she would be counting. Zax only dipped his head. But as he turned, his eyes met T'Vella's, and the Vulcan and the Andorian, who had not agreed about a single thing in two years on one bridge, appeared to have agreed about this. The captain had said *careful*. Between them they meant to see it stayed more than a word.

"Kenji," Eva said. "Take us in. Low and quiet. Stay where I can see you."

"Aye, Captain!"

He went up through the branches and turned east, against stars that had no names. The rest of them went after him.

They ran. The bodies knew the dark better than the minds that rode them: an Absol could cross broken ground at night like water going downhill, and a Lucario could keep pace with her and never put a foot wrong. Anya thundered behind with her fire banked low. T'Vella did not seem to run so much as arrive, and Elara, too small for this forest, ran flat out in the middle of them all and did not fall behind.

Two thin moons stood over the trees. Somewhere ahead, past a ridge they could not see yet, a town had its lamps lit.

Six officers of a ship that no longer existed ran toward it through the dark.
