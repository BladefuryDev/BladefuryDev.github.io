The darkness of the forest was absolute. The twin moons of this world were little more than slivers in the sky, offering no comfort against the blackness, and the stars between them were the wrong stars. The crew of the former U.S.S. Sagittarius pushed onward, their only light being the mournful, flickering flames on Anya's neck.

For the first hour the ground was the blast's. Trees lay where the shockwave had put them, all pointing the same way, their torn roots toward the crater and their crowns away from it, so that walking away from the wreck meant following the grain of a whole fallen forest, trunk after trunk aimed like arrows at wherever they were going. They climbed over trunks wider than a shuttle. They went under others on their bellies, Anya last, holding her breath and her fire. Twice the way was simply blocked, a wall of fallen timber too high and too tangled, and they went around in the dark and lost whatever line they had been holding.

Then, slowly, the fallen trees began to lean rather than lie. Then some stood. Then the forest was a forest again, the undergrowth thick and damp and whole, and the only sign of what had happened behind them was a faint glow on the undersides of the clouds to the west, which none of them looked at.

Anya walked with her head bowed, feeding the fire on her neck as little as she dared. Every time her attention slipped, it flared, and the leaves it touched hissed and curled, and she pulled it back down with a grunt of effort that was starting to sound like pain. "It wants to be lit," she said, the third time it happened. "It's like holding a breath. It's like holding a breath for an hour."

"Then let it out somewhere it can't hurt anything," Eva said. "And tell me when you need to."

Exhaustion was a physical weight on each of them, a deep, bone-weary fatigue that went beyond simple tiredness. Their new bodies, while resilient to the shockwave, were now screaming for rest and sustenance. A gnawing hunger twisted in their stomachs, a primal need their human forms had rarely experienced outside of survival training. A desperate, rasping thirst made their thoughts sluggish and their tempers short. Every stumble on a hidden root, every vine that snagged a leg, was met with a low growl or a frustrated sigh.

And the bodies themselves were not cooperating.

Eva had four legs and no idea what to do with them. Walking was fine, walking she had worked out in the first ten minutes, some old deep part of the body taking it over for her. It was everything else. She would lift a forepaw to step over a root and the hind leg on the same side would not follow, and she would go down on her chin in the leaf litter with a yowl she had not meant to make. The horn was worse. It caught on everything. Twice she had turned her head to look at Zax and hooked a vine and dragged half a bush along with her. And there was the tail, which she kept forgetting entirely until it struck something, and which seemed to have opinions.

Elara's problem was size. She had been a grown woman of ordinary height. She was now a creature that came up to Zax's hip, in a forest built to the scale of the fallen giants behind them, where a fern was a wall and a root was a step as high as her chest. She did not complain once. Eva noticed that she had started, without a word, to walk in Anya's footprints, where the big paws had already crushed a path.

Kenji could not land. He could fly, badly, in short frantic bursts from branch to branch, but every landing ended the same way, with his talons grabbing for a perch that his body had not finished arriving at, and a scrabble, and a fall, and an indignant chirp from the undergrowth. The fourth time, Zax caught him without looking, one paw closing gently around the small body on the way down, and set him on his shoulder. "Stay," he said. Kenji stayed.

Zax himself was walking like a man wearing someone else's boots. His legs were shorter than they should have been and far stronger, and he kept overstepping, driving a paw into the ground hard enough to leave a print. He said nothing about it. He had said very little about anything since the crater. His ears, the new ones, never stopped moving.

"Captain, we must find water," T'Vella said, her melodic voice strained, a crack in her usual Vulcan composure. "I have no instruments, but the signs do not require any. Dry mouths. Slowed reactions. Captain, you have fallen four times in the last ten minutes, and it is no longer only the legs. We are dehydrating, and these bodies appear to burn through their reserves quickly."

"I'm aware, T'Vella," Eva replied, her voice sharp with strain. Her Absol paws, not designed for long-distance trekking, were sore and tender. The sickle-shaped horn on her head proved useful for slicing through thick vines, but it was slow, exhausting work. "This terrain is... difficult."

"Difficult is an understatement," Zax grunted from behind her, his powerful Lucario legs aching from the constant, uneven pace. "We're moving at a crawl. We need to find a defensible position and make camp."

"And where do you suggest we do that?" Eva snapped back, then immediately regretted her tone. She took a breath. "I'm trying. Kenji, see anything from up there?"

The Starly launched himself from Zax's shoulder and fluttered up through the branches, his small body barely a silhouette against the star-dusted sky. He was gone longer than Eva liked. When he came down, he came down badly, and Zax caught him again. "It's all the same, Captain! Trees and more trees! I can't see a thing!" he chirped, his voice high with anxiety. Then, quieter: "Captain. I looked up, too. I don't know any of them."

"Any of what?"

"The stars." He had been a navigator before he had been a pilot; it was the first thing Starfleet taught you to be. "I know every sky from here to the Expanse. I know what Althos looks like from the outside. There's nothing up there I know. Not one." His feathers had puffed out, all over, and he could not seem to make them lie flat. "We're not anywhere, Captain. We're not anywhere I have a chart for."

Nobody answered him, because there was nothing to say, and because Zax had stopped.

The Lucario stood perfectly still, his new, pointed ears swiveling towards a sound in the undergrowth. "Hold," he commanded, his voice a low growl. He held up a paw for silence. A faint rustle to their left. Then another to their right. A soft chirp from the branches above. The sounds were all around them.

The others froze, their own new senses straining against the dark. T'Vella's head tilted with a faint curiosity. "The sounds are not aggressive," she noted quietly. "They are... curious."

Anya instinctively flared the flames on her neck brighter, and for once was glad of them, casting the group in a wider circle of wavering, orange light. The sudden illumination revealed dozens of pairs of glowing eyes watching them from the shadows of the massive trees. Small, rodent-like creatures, owl-like birds, and insectoid beings stared back, not with aggression, but with a palpable sense of wonder.

"They're just watching us," Elara, the Kirlia, whispered, her own large eyes wide.

"They are waiting," T'Vella said. "Observe. None of them has moved since the light. They are not deciding whether to approach. They are watching to see what we do." She considered the eyes with the detachment of a scientist on the wrong side of the glass. "It is, I suspect, what we would do."

"Let's not give them a show," Eva decided. "We need to make camp. Now." She dropped the portable shelter emitter to the ground. "Anya, give me a hand."

Anya tapped the cracked screen of the tricorder, her large claws surprisingly deft. "The emitter's power cell is at seventeen percent. On low output it might see us to morning. No further." She crouched and touched a control on the emitter itself. With a low hum, a shimmering, dome-shaped energy field expanded outwards, creating a six-meter bubble of clear, protected space. The strange creatures of the forest retreated from its edge, their glowing eyes still fixed upon the newcomers.

The sense of relief inside the bubble was immediate. The darkness seemed to recede, and the air felt safer.

"While it's up," Anya said, "I want the rest of the readings." She turned the tricorder on the night, slowly, the way she would have swept a damaged compartment, and read out what it gave her in the flat voice of someone filing a report because the alternative was thinking. "Atmosphere: nitrogen-oxygen, a shade richer than Earth standard. Gravity close to one g. Temperature twelve degrees and falling. Planetary magnetic field, strong, stable." She paused. "Two moons, both up. I've run the sky against the stellar catalogue three times. Nothing matches. Nothing matches at all." She lowered the device. "He's right. We're not anywhere."

"We are somewhere," T'Vella said. "We simply do not yet know where. The distinction matters, Chief."

"Does it?"

"It is the difference between a problem and a mystery. A problem can be solved."

Her gaze had drifted past Anya while she spoke, and it had stopped. "And I have found something," she said, in a different voice, and pointed toward a patch of thick, leafy bushes at the edge of their camp, just inside the dome. Growing from them were clusters of large, blue berries that seemed to glow with a faint inner light. She looked at them for a long moment. "They appear... appetizing," she said, and sounded, for the first time any of them could remember, unsure of her own sentence. "I do not know why I know that. I have never seen them. And yet the knowledge that they are food is... present. As if it were already mine."

"Appetizing?" Zax grunted, eyeing the berries with suspicion. "We don't know what they are."

"Let me see," Anya said, hobbling over with the tricorder. She scanned the berries, her brow furrowed in concentration as she interpreted the damaged device's readings. "No toxins I can identify. The cellular structure is... bizarre, but the chemical composition appears safe for carbon-based life. It's a risk, but a calculated one." She glanced at T'Vella. "And I'd like to know how you knew that before I did."

"So would I," T'Vella said.

Eva ate first. It was not bravery; it was the job. She bit into one of the berries and stood there with the juice running down her chin, waiting to find out if she had just poisoned herself, and after a slow count of thirty she said, "It's fine," and the others fell on the bush.

The taste was explosive, a sweet, hydrating burst of flavor unlike anything they had ever experienced. It was both food and water, and it was the most delicious thing any of them had ever tasted. For a few minutes, there was only the sound of grateful eating. Kenji ate until he fell over. Anya ate with her eyes closed. Elara, who had to climb the bush to reach, ate sitting in it.

With their immediate thirst and hunger sated, the reality of their situation settled back in, heavier than before. The brief respite only served to highlight the sheer insanity of their circumstances. They huddled together in the center of the shimmering dome, six Starfleet officers in the bodies of monsters, lost in a world that defied every natural law they knew. The silence was broken only by the crackle of Anya's flames and the distant, alien sounds of the forest.

"These bodies..." Zax finally said, looking at his own paws, which ended in sharp, white claws. He flexed the paw, and the claws slid back into it and were gone, and he flexed it again, and they came out. He had been doing that, on and off, for an hour. "It's a strange mixture," he said. "The parts that should be a hand aren't quite. The parts that should be a weapon are." He paused, testing those claws once more. "They are strong. Resilient. But they are not ours. I feel... an anger that is not my own simmering beneath the surface."

"I feel it too," Anya agreed, her voice low. "A heat. A constant, burning fire that wants to be let loose. It takes all my concentration to keep it from raging out of control." She looked at her own hulking form, so different from the human woman she had been just hours ago. "I'm an engineer. I build things. What am I supposed to do with... this?"

"I am cold," T'Vella said, as if continuing a list she had been keeping. "Or this body reports cold, which is not the same thing. I have no instrument to say which. My heart rate is slower; that much I can count. My field of vision is wider. My hands have three fingers, which I have been attempting not to think about. And I am experiencing an emotion I can only describe as *lightness*, which has no cause, and which I do not trust."

"Everything's too big," Elara said. It was almost a whisper. "That's all. I keep reaching for things and they're not where my hand goes."

"I keep fluffing up," Kenji said miserably, from where he lay on his side in the grass. "I can't stop it. Every time something scares me I turn into a ball." A pause. "It's happening right now."

Eva said nothing about her own body. She had sat down, finally, and the tail had curled itself around her paws without being asked, and she had decided to allow that.

"What do we do tomorrow, Captain?" Kenji chirped, his small voice cutting through the heavy atmosphere. He had dragged himself over to huddle against Zax's leg, seeking warmth and protection from a world that had suddenly become terrifyingly large. "We have no ship. No long-range comms. No Starfleet."

Eva looked at the faces of her crew, illuminated by the soft glow of the shelter and Anya's flames. She saw their fear, their confusion, their grief. She felt it too, a cold, hard knot in her own stomach. But she was their Captain. Her own despair had to wait.

"Tomorrow," she said, her voice steady and sure, "we establish a new protocol. First, we find a defensible position with a steady source of water. Second, we calibrate. We learn the capabilities and limitations of these new bodies. We treat them as unfamiliar equipment and run full diagnostics." She looked at T'Vella's three-fingered hands, folded in her lap. "Every one of us has just given me a fault report. Good. That's how it starts. Third, we survive. And fourth, we search for answers. That is our mission now."

"And tonight?" Zax asked.

"Tonight we sleep. Two-hour watches. You have the first."

"I'll take the first and the second."

"You'll take the first," Eva said, "and you'll wake Anya for the second, and I'll know if you don't."

He looked at her for a moment, and then, for the first time since the crater, something in his face eased. "Aye, Captain."

One by one, they settled, as well as bodies that had never slept could settle. Anya lay down with her back to the others so her flames faced the dark, and banked them, and the light in the dome went from orange to a dim, steady red. Elara curled up small against her side, in the warmth. Kenji stayed where he was, against Zax's leg, and was asleep before anyone else. T'Vella sat upright, her hands in her lap, and closed her eyes, and whether that was sleep or something else, no one could have said.

Eva laid her head on her paws, but her eyes remained open, staring out into the darkness beyond their fragile bubble of safety. Sleep would not come easily. The faces of her lost crew, the sound of the ship tearing itself apart, the final, silent flash of the warp core, the world on the viewscreen, too close: it all played on a loop in her mind.

Zax stood at the edge of the dome with his back to her, watching the eyes. After a while, without turning around, he said quietly, "You should sleep, Captain."

"So should you."

"I'm on watch."

"So am I."

He did not argue. When his two hours were up he woke Anya, exactly as ordered, and lay down, and Eva could tell from his breathing that he did not sleep either. The night went on. The watchers in the trees began, one pair of eyes at a time, to lose interest and blink out. By the time the first grey came into the sky there was only one pair left, small and low and patient, and when the light reached it, it too was gone. Eva heard it go: a quick patter of small feet through the leaf litter, not wandering off the way the others had, but running, straight and purposeful, deeper into the woods. As if it had somewhere to be. As if it had someone to tell.

She could not afford to break. Not now. Not while five other lives depended on her. They had survived their first night. The dawn would bring with it a whole new set of unknowns, in a world where they were the aliens.
